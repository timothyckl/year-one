# Technical Proposal Structure

## The Full Structure

| Part | What it contains |
|---|---|
| Front matter | Cover page; table of contents; executive summary |
| Introduction | Background paragraph; problem statement; purpose statement; scope |
| Literature review | Synthesis and critical evaluation of past research |
| Body | Proposed improvement and rationale; objectives; benefits; sustainability balance; adaptability |
| Methods | Justified methods for evaluating efficiency and sustainability |
| Conclusion | Summary of the main argument; take-home message |
| Back matter | References; appendices |

The counted span from the Introduction through the Conclusion must not exceed
2000 words. The executive summary, references, and appendices are not part of
the word count, but they are still assessed.

## Front Matter

- Cover page: the institution's logo, the formal name of the assignment (as
  stated in the assignment brief), the name of your proposal, the names of the
  team members with their matriculation numbers, and the date of completion.
- Table of contents: lists the executive summary, the main content, and the
  back matter including the reference page; easy to read and accurately
  formatted.
- Executive summary: written last; around 200 words; an opening hook, the
  problem / need, an overview of the proposed solution, and a short conclusion
  with a call to action.

## Problem Analysis Tools

- Gap analysis: compare the present state with a desired state and determine
  the steps needed to improve. A gap analysis flow diagram shows whether
  strategic objectives are being met, barely met, or not met.
- SWOT analysis: examine the strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats
  relevant to a problem or solution.
- Fishbone diagram (root-cause analysis): the problem goes in the fish's head,
  and the causes go on the spines.
- The 5 Whys and the McKinsey 7S framework can also be used to probe problems.

## Writing The Problem Statement

- Why it matters: the problem statement is often glossed over as if the problem
  is general knowledge; a lack of context creates gaps in the reader's
  understanding.
- Content: the current situation, and the limitations in the current situation
  / root causes of the problem.
- Sources: primary (the analysis tools above) and secondary (the literature
  review).

## Writing Objectives

- Align with the statement of the problem.
- State clearly and specifically; identify what will be measured to ascertain
  the effectiveness of the proposed solution.
- May include the anticipated benefits if the solution is implemented
  successfully.

## The Introduction

- Purpose: provide background, establish the significance of the study, and
  reveal the objective.
- Components: the background paragraph (use the PEEL framework), the problem
  statement paragraph (context and relevance), the purpose statement, and the
  scope.
- Purpose statement quality: specific and precise, concise (max two sentences),
  clear, and goal-oriented. It should flow directly from the problem statement.
- Scope: what will be discussed (and reasonably what will not); an overview of
  needs and desired outcomes; and the limitations, to give a realistic
  understanding of the proposed strategy.

## The Literature Review

- Review paragraphs follow a claim + evidence structure.
- Common strategies: contrast interpretations; show connections between source
  texts; show connections between the literature and the current study; point
  out limitations and potential; note the scope of previous research and the
  niche of the current study; point out weaknesses.
- Fallacies to avoid: faulty conclusions from skeletal evidence; ignoring
  alternatives; appeals to emotion; appeals to ignorance; misplaced causality;
  begging the question; disconnected conclusions without evidence.

## The Body

- Proposed improvement(s): what modification, solution, or change is proposed;
  the rationale and specific objectives; the key components; the technology
  (hardware, software, or network) as understood at this time; and a diagram,
  graph, or picture illustrating the problems and proposed solutions.
- Benefits: explain the anticipated benefits from the viewpoints of key
  stakeholders.
- Sustainability: discuss planet, people, profit, and principles; explain how
  these dimensions are balanced fairly; compare that balance with the current
  system, design, process, procedure, or protocol.
- Adaptability: explain changes that may be necessary in response to new
  developments or disruptions involving technology, climate, the economy,
  politics, or social values.

## Methods

- Elaborate on and justify methods for evaluating the efficiency and
  sustainability of the improved system.
- Consider how the improvement will be assessed, possible unintended
  consequences or costs, and the viewpoints of varied stakeholders.

## The Conclusion

- Sum up the main argument and the key points of each main section.
- Relay the writer's key take-home message.
- End with a strong final impression of significance within a broader context.
- Do not introduce new information or new ideas.

## Language And Style

- Use the active voice wherever possible.
- Use precise verbs instead of abstract nouns.
- Use concrete terms.
- Use positive words instead of negative ones for persuasiveness.
- Avoid jargon, statements that cannot be backed up with evidence, cliches, and
  buzzwords.
- Cite sources using APA 7th edition; check that 6th edition rules are not used,
  especially for books and online resources.

## Appendices

- Contain additional details or relevant information referred to in the
  proposal (graphs, tables, survey results, and similar material).
- Label them (for example, Appendix A, Appendix B).
- Not included in the word count.

## Formatting And Proofreading

- Follow the formatting instructions given in the brief.
- Proofread for coherence and cohesion, correct grammar, and clear sentence
  structure.
