# Literature Review Guide

## Purpose

The literature review summarizes, synthesizes, and critically evaluates past
research that is relevant to the problem and to the proposed solution. It
establishes what is known, what is missing, and where the current study fits.

## Paragraph Structure

The review paragraph usually follows a claim + evidence structure: state the
point you are trying to prove, then provide the proof.

## Review Strategies

Use one or more of these strategies to write a critical review:

- contrasting two different interpretations;
- showing the connections between different source texts;
- showing the connections between the literature and the current study;
- pointing out the limitations and potential of different theories;
- pointing out the scope of previous research and the niche of the current
  study;
- pointing out the weaknesses of a particular type of study.

## Organizing The Review

- Organize the review by subtopic or theme (for example, data accuracy,
  personalisation of data, incentives).
- Within each theme, compare how the findings of different studies agree,
  disagree, or build on each other.
- Trace developments over time where useful, showing how later studies respond
  to earlier ones.

## Fallacies To Avoid

- Jumping to a faulty conclusion based on skeletal evidence or an incomplete
  evaluation of the evidence.
- Presenting a conclusion without properly addressing other alternatives.
- Appealing to emotions rather than evidence.
- Appealing to ignorance, assuming a claim is true because it has not been
  proven false.
- Misplaced causality, arguing from cause to effect or effect to cause.
- Begging the question, asserting a claim and then using the same claim as its
  own evidence.
- A disconnected conclusion without any evidence to support it.

## Critical Evaluation: The Author's Voice

A review reflects your critical interpretation and evaluation of past research,
not just a summary. Show the author's voice through evaluative language:

- point out what the arguments adequately engage and what they miss;
- identify the advantage of a work and why it matters for your purposes;
- highlight the systemic changes a study identifies;
- note where interpretations overlook important considerations.

## Using The Paul-Elder Framework

Apply the elements of thought and intellectual standards to check the reasoning
in your literature review. The Proposal Evaluation Worksheet provides the
criteria for each element (purpose, question at issue, information, inferences,
concepts, assumptions, implications, points of view).

## Language Conventions

- Use the active voice wherever possible (for example, "they found", not "it
  was determined").
- Use precise verbs instead of abstract nouns (for example, "extract", not
  "engage in the extraction of").
- Use concrete terms (for example, "workers", not "human resources").
- Use positive words instead of negative ones (for example, "possible", not
  "not impossible").
- Avoid jargon or technical language the reader may not understand.
- Avoid statements that cannot be backed up with evidence.
- Avoid cliches and buzzwords such as "ground-breaking", "cutting edge",
  "world class", or "win-win".
