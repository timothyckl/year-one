# Week 6: Preparing the Technical Proposal

## Overview

A proposal is a persuasive document. Its objectives are to:

- define what a project is about;
- explain why this project is important and makes a difference to the current
  situation;
- convince the reader that you have a well thought-through technical approach.

A proposal is a very important document you may produce during your studies and
your career in high-stakes situations, such as grant applications and business
deals. In this module you learn to write a proposal as well as the necessary
communication skills related to it, particularly the skill to communicate
complex ideas clearly, concisely, and critically.

## Information Elements In A Proposal

A proposal carries a set of information elements. These map onto the DMAIC
stages (Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control), with the main written
elements presented under the Define stage:

| DMAIC stage | Information elements in the proposal |
|---|---|
| Define the problem | Problem statement; root causes of the problem; objectives including anticipated benefits; solutions that can be considered; the approach you are recommending; the novelty of the approach recommended; arguments to support the approach recommended; technical approach / methodology (how you will measure the effectiveness of the solution); conclusion |
| Measure / quantify the problem | |
| Analyze the cause of the problem | |
| Improve / implement and verify the solution | |
| Control / maintain the solution | |

## The Problem Statement: The "Why?"

The statement of the problem is often glossed over, as if the problem is
general knowledge. The lack of a context or background for the problem creates
gaps in a reader's understanding of your proposal.

The statement of the problem generally has the following sections:

- current situation;
- limitations in the current situation / root causes of the problem.

Information sources:

- primary information source: tools to analyze issues in the current process
  (gap analysis, fishbone diagram, 5 Whys);
- secondary information source: the literature review (covered in Week 8).

## Tools To Analyze Or Probe Problems

There are many tools you can use to define, measure, and analyze problems. The
module introduces three:

### Gap Analysis

Gap analysis is a simple method of comparing the present state of something to
a desired state, and then determining the steps that must be taken to improve
the state. A gap analysis flow diagram reflects the gaps in the process, that
is, whether strategic objectives are being met, barely met, or not met at all.
It highlights to readers the issues that need to be addressed.

### SWOT Analysis

SWOT analysis examines the strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats
relevant to a problem or a solution.

### Fishbone Diagram (Root-Cause Analysis)

A fishbone diagram is an established problem-solving tool. In a fishbone
diagram, the problem is stated in the fish's head, and the causes of the
problem are placed on the spines of the fishbone.

## Objectives: The "What?"

The objective(s) of the project must align with the statement of the problem.

- State your project objectives clearly and in a specific manner.
- The reader needs to know which issue is the focus of the proposal and what is
  being measured to ascertain the effectiveness of the proposed solution.
- You could also include a statement of the anticipated benefits if the solution
  is implemented successfully.

If the statement of the problem is the "why" of your project, the objectives
are the "what".

## Proposed Improvements

- Must be based on the analysis of the problem presented.
- Must reflect that they are well thought-through: feasible, and considerate of
  multiple perspectives of the issue from stakeholders' viewpoints and the
  solutions available in the market.
- Best presented graphically to enhance clarity.

## Methodology

The methodology explains how the effectiveness of the improved process will be
measured (for example, through surveys, observation, or quantifiable data
collected before and after implementation).

## Homework

Work with your group on the MIRO board. Think about:

- What are you proposing in your project? What is the novelty or appeal of the
  approach recommended?
- The significance of your idea in terms of outcome. How would this be
  attractive and beneficial?

Examples of outcome-oriented statements:

- Adding this new feature will increase the accuracy of location tracking and
  will therefore provide a better estimate of waiting time for customers
  waiting for their private-ride hail.
- Changing the design of the user interface will make it more user-friendly for
  older users, as the new design is streamlined and customizable.
