# Week 1 - Critical Thinking and the Paul-Elder Framework

## Overview

This unit introduces the module's two central concerns: critical thinking and
communication. It explains why both matter at university and in the workplace,
introduces the growth mindset as a foundation for learning, and presents the
Paul-Elder framework, which is used throughout the module to structure,
analyse, and assess thinking.

## Why Critical Thinking and Communication Matter

- Critical thinking is central to any credible educational journey, especially
  one focused on communication skills. Every activity and extended task in this
  module is done with critical thinking concepts and strategies highlighted.
- Communication is essential for leadership and for projects: a message that is
  not delivered clearly and does not motivate action may as well not exist.
- Employers identify communication as one of the basic competencies every
  graduate should have; it is valuable for obtaining employment and maintaining
  successful job performance.
- Throughout the module, critical thinking skills are made more visible as you
  work through assignment briefs, reviews, and consultations.

## Learning Outcomes For This Unit

After working through this unit you should be able to:

- recognise and explain the importance of communication and critical thinking;
- understand how the knowledge and skills in this module can help you refine
  your critical thinking and communication;
- apply the Paul-Elder framework for critical thinking;
- explain how a growth mindset can help your learning journey;
- set your own learning goals for the module.

## Growth Mindset

A growth mindset is the belief that abilities can be developed through effort,
good strategies, and learning from mistakes, rather than being fixed at birth.
Being an active learner and developing this mindset is a great advantage.

Points to discuss and reflect on:

- The role of grit (passion and perseverance) in completing challenging tasks.
- Embracing vulnerability and learning from falling down and making mistakes.
- Making learning enjoyable: "smart people do what they like; wise people do
  what needs to be done in an enjoyable way."
- Strategies you use to motivate yourself and to initiate and sustain
  engagement in learning activities.
- Building a personal list of strategies to use when you feel disengaged.

## The Paul-Elder Framework

A working definition of critical thinking: the mode of thinking in which the
thinker improves the quality of their thinking by skilfully taking charge of
the structures inherent in thinking and imposing intellectual standards upon
them.

The framework has three components:

1. **Elements of Thought** - the structures that make up reasoning.
2. **Intellectual Standards** - the criteria used to assess the quality of
   reasoning.
3. **Intellectual Traits** - the virtues cultivated by routinely applying the
   standards.

## Component 1: Elements of Thought

The eight elements are the parts present in all reasoning. For each element,
there are checking questions that help you interrogate your own or someone
else's thinking.

### Point of View

The "place" from which you view something: what you are looking at and the way
you are seeing it. Consider the limitations of your point of view and other
relevant viewpoints.

Questions to check your point of view:

- How am I looking at this situation? Is there another way to look at it?
- What exactly am I focused on, and how am I seeing it?
- Is my view the only reasonable view? What does my point of view ignore?
- Have I considered how others (for example, people in a different situation)
  view this?
- Which of these possible viewpoints makes the most sense given the situation?
- Am I having difficulty looking at this from a viewpoint with which I
  disagree?

### Purpose

Your goal, objective, or what you are trying to accomplish. This also includes
functions, motives, and intentions. Your purpose should be clear and
justifiable.

Questions which target purpose:

- What is the purpose in doing this?
- What is the objective of this assignment, task, experiment, project, or
  strategy?
- Should we question, refine, or modify our purpose?
- What is the purpose of this meeting, chapter, relationship, or action?
- What is your central aim in this line of thought?

### Question At Issue

The question lays out the problem or issue and guides the thinking. A vague
question leads to unclear thinking, so the question should be clear and precise
enough to productively guide reasoning.

Questions which target the question:

- What is the question I am trying to answer?
- What important questions are embedded in the issue?
- Is there a better way to put the question? Is it clear? Is it complex?
- What kind of question is this: historical, scientific, ethical, political,
  economic, or something else?

### Information

The facts, data, evidence, or experiences used to figure things out.
Information does not necessarily imply accuracy, so the information you use
should be accurate and relevant to the question or issue.

Questions which target information:

- What information do I need to answer this question?
- What data are relevant to this problem?
- Do we need to gather more information? Is this information relevant to our
  purpose or goal?
- On what information are you basing that comment?
- What experience convinced you of this? Could your experience be distorted?
- Have we left out any important information that needs to be considered?

### Inferences

Inferences are interpretations or conclusions you come to. Inferring is what
the mind does in figuring something out. Inferences should logically follow
from the evidence; infer no more or no less than what is implied.

Questions to check your inferences:

- What conclusions am I coming to? Are there other conclusions I should
  consider?
- Is my inference logical? Does this interpretation make sense?
- Does our solution necessarily follow from our data?
- How did you reach that conclusion? What are you basing your reasoning on?
- Is there an alternative plausible conclusion?
- Given all the facts, what is the best possible conclusion?

### Concepts

Concepts are the ideas, theories, laws, principles, or hypotheses we use in
thinking to make sense of things. Be clear about the concepts you use and use
them justifiably.

Questions you can ask about concepts:

- What idea am I using in my thinking? Is this idea causing problems?
- I think this is a good theory, but could you explain it more fully?
- What is the main hypothesis you are using in your reasoning?
- What main distinctions should we draw in reasoning through this problem?
- What idea is this author using in their thinking? Is there a problem with it?
- How shall we interpret these data?

### Assumptions

Assumptions are beliefs you take for granted. They usually operate at the
subconscious or unconscious level of thought. Be clear about your assumptions
and make sure they are justified by sound evidence.

Questions you can ask about assumptions:

- What am I assuming or taking for granted?
- Am I assuming something I should not?
- What assumption is leading me to this conclusion?
- What is this policy, strategy, or explanation assuming?
- What does this field of study take for granted?
- What is being presupposed in this theory?

### Implications And Consequences

Implications are claims or truths that logically follow from other claims or
truths. Implications follow from thoughts; consequences follow from actions.
Implications are inherent in your thoughts whether you see them or not, so the
best thinkers think through the logical implications before acting.

Questions you can ask about implications:

- If I decide to do X, what things might happen?
- If I decide not to do X, what things might happen?
- What are you implying when you say that?
- What is likely to happen if we do this versus that?
- How significant are the implications of this decision?

### Remembering The Eight Elements

When we think, we think for a **purpose**, within a **point of view**, based on
**assumptions**, leading to **implications and consequences**, using **data,
facts, and experiences**, to make **inferences and interpretations**, based on
**concepts and theories**, to address a **question, issue, or problem**.

## Component 2: Intellectual Standards

Effective thinkers go beyond taking thinking apart: they also apply standards
to the elements of thought to assess the quality of their thinking. The goal is
for these standards to become infused in your thinking, forming part of your
inner voice and guiding you to assess, evaluate, reason, and construct better
arguments.

The framework promotes a set of intellectual standards. The main standards used
to evaluate reasoning are:

| Standard | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Clarity | Is the idea clear enough to be understood? |
| Accuracy | Is the information true and free from error? |
| Precision | Is the idea specific and exact enough? |
| Relevance | Does the information connect to the question at issue? |
| Depth | Does the reasoning address the complexity of the issue? |
| Breadth | Are alternative points of view considered? |
| Logic | Does the reasoning follow in a sensible way? |
| Significance | Is the focus on what matters most? |
| Sufficiency (completeness) | Is there enough information, and is anything necessary missing from the reasoning? |
| Fairness | Is the reasoning free from bias and self-interest? |

### Criteria For Evaluating Reasoning

| Element of thought | Criteria questions |
|---|---|
| Purpose | Is the purpose clearly stated or clearly implied? Is it significant? |
| Question at issue | Is the question well-stated? Is it clear and unbiased? Does it do justice to the complexity of the matter? Is the question relevant to the purpose? |
| Information | Does the writer cite relevant evidence, experiences, or information essential to the issue? Is the information accurate? Does it address the complexities of the issue? |
| Inferences and interpretations | Does the writer develop a line of reasoning that explains how they arrive at their main conclusions? |
| Concepts | Does the writer clarify key concepts when necessary? Are the concepts used accurately? |
| Assumptions | Does the writer show sensitivity to what they are taking for granted? Are questionable assumptions addressed? |
| Implications and consequences | Does the writer show sensitivity to the implications and consequences of the position they take? |
| Points of view | Does the writer show sensitivity to alternative relevant points of view or lines of reasoning? Does the writer consider and respond to objections? |

## Component 3: Intellectual Traits

The routine, consistent, and deliberate application of intellectual standards
to reasoning cultivates intellectual virtues. These are the habits of mind that
critical thinkers develop.

| Trait | What it means | Reflective question |
|---|---|---|
| Intellectual humility | Awareness of the limits of your own knowledge | What do I really know about this issue? Do my prejudices, attitudes, or experiences bias my judgment? Am I quick to admit when I am beyond my expertise? |
| Intellectual courage | Willingness to examine your own beliefs and stand by well-reasoned positions | Have I analysed the beliefs I hold that may impede my thinking? Am I willing to yield my position when evidence is against it? Am I willing to stand my ground against the majority? |
| Intellectual empathy | Understanding others' reasoning and viewpoints | Do I listen and seek to understand others' reasoning? Do I accurately represent viewpoints I disagree with? |
| Intellectual integrity | Holding yourself to the same standards as others | Do I expect of myself what I expect of others? Do I strive to eliminate self-deception or self-interest in reasoning? |
| Intellectual perseverance | Working through complexity rather than giving up | Am I willing to work through the complexities of an issue, or do I give up when challenged? |
| Confidence in reason | Trusting evidence and reasoning over coercion | Am I willing to change my position when the evidence leads to a more reasonable position? Do I encourage others to reach their own conclusions? |
| Intellectual autonomy | Thinking through issues for yourself | Do I think through technical issues on my own, or do I merely accept others' conclusions? Am I willing to stand alone against irrational criticism? |
| Fairmindedness | Giving dissenting views adequate consideration | Am I giving dissenting opinions adequate consideration? Has self-interest or bias clouded my judgment? Have I thought through potential hazards to others? |

## Applying The Framework

Consider the following claim and evaluate it using each component of the
framework:

> AI algorithms are completely unbiased and objective decision-makers. By
> relying on data-driven insights, AI can eliminate human biases and deliver
> fair and equitable outcomes in all situations.

Questions to work through:

- **Elements:** What is the purpose of this claim? What question is it
  answering? What assumptions does it make (for example, about the data, the
  algorithms, or the meaning of "fair")? What information would be needed to
  support or refute it? What inferences does the author draw, and do they
  follow from the evidence? What point of view is being presented? What are the
  implications if the claim is correct or incorrect?
- **Standards:** Is the claim clear, accurate, and precise? Does it address the
  complexity of AI and bias? Are alternative points of view considered? Is the
  reasoning logical and fair?
- **Traits:** Would a fair-minded thinker accept "completely" and "in all
  situations" without further evidence? What would intellectual humility
  require you to check here?
