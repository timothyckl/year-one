## The Paul-Elder Framework

Critical thinking: taking charge of the structures inherent in thinking and
imposing intellectual standards upon them.

Three components:

1. **Elements of Thought** - the structures of reasoning.
2. **Intellectual Standards** - criteria for judging reasoning.
3. **Intellectual Traits** - habits cultivated by applying the standards.

### Eight Elements Of Thought

| Element | Definition |
|---|---|
| Purpose | The goal or objective you are trying to accomplish |
| Question at issue | The problem or issue that guides the thinking |
| Information | Facts, data, evidence, or experiences used |
| Inferences | Interpretations or conclusions drawn from the information |
| Concepts | Ideas, theories, laws, principles, or hypotheses used |
| Assumptions | Beliefs taken for granted |
| Implications and consequences | What logically follows from the thinking and action |
| Point of view | The frame from which you view the issue |

**Mnemonic:** We think for a purpose, within a point of view, based on
assumptions, leading to implications and consequences, using data, facts, and
experiences, to make inferences and interpretations, based on concepts and
theories, to address a question, issue, or problem.

### Intellectual Standards

- Clarity, accuracy, precision, relevance, depth, breadth, logic, significance,
  sufficiency/completeness, fairness.

### Intellectual Traits

- Intellectual humility, intellectual courage, intellectual empathy,
  intellectual integrity, intellectual perseverance, confidence in reason,
  intellectual autonomy, fairmindedness.

### Evaluating Reasoning - Key Questions

- Purpose: Is it clearly stated and significant?
- Question: Is it well-stated, clear, unbiased, and relevant to the purpose?
- Information: Is it relevant, accurate, and sufficient for the complexity?
- Inferences: Is there a line of reasoning to the conclusions?
- Concepts: Are key concepts clarified and used accurately?
- Assumptions: Is the writer aware of what they take for granted?
- Implications: Is there sensitivity to consequences of the position?
- Points of view: Are alternative relevant viewpoints considered and answered?

## Systems Thinking

Three concepts for analysis, evaluation, and decision-making:

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Interconnection | The parts of a system affect one another |
| Big picture | Understand a system in its wider context, not just one metric |
| Feedback loops | Cycles where an outcome influences the behaviour that produced it |

- **Reinforcing virtuous cycle**: success builds on success (reliable buses ->
  more riders -> more investment -> better service -> more riders).
- **Reinforcing vicious cycle**: decline feeds decline (unreliable service ->
  fewer riders -> less funding -> worse service).
- **Balancing loop**: counteracts change and moves the system towards stability.

**Self-check questions:** Has the author considered interconnection, the big
picture, and feedback loops? Am I seeing connections? Am I thinking big picture?
What feedback loops might this create?

## Academic Writing

### Paragraph Structure

- **Topic sentence**: topic + controlling idea; neither too broad nor too
  narrow.
- **Supporting information**: complete, sufficient, and relevant details that
  develop the controlling idea (examples, facts, and other evidence).
- **Concluding sentence**: restate the main idea, give final thoughts or
  implications; never introduce a new idea.

### Unity, Coherence, Cohesion

- **Unity**: one idea from start to finish; every sentence contributes.
- **Coherence**: logical order of ideas; last sentence connects to the topic
  sentence.
- **Cohesion**: smooth connection of ideas using cohesive devices (transitions,
  conjunctions, reference words).

### Sentence Types

| Type | Structure |
|---|---|
| Simple | One main clause |
| Compound | Two main clauses joined by and, but, or, yet |
| Complex | Main clause + subordinate clause (when, because, while) |
| Compound-complex | Two or more independent clauses + one dependent clause |

### Tone, Style, Voice

- Tones include formal, informal, neutral, persuasive, authoritative, humorous.
- Styles include narrative, descriptive, expository, persuasive, argumentative,
  technical, creative.
- The passive voice is more common in formal and academic writing; it moves
  focus away from the doer.

### Hedges, Boosters, Reporting Verbs

| Device | Purpose | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Hedges | Soften claims, show caution | possibly, likely, perhaps, it is suggested, suggest, indicate, propose, argue, claim |
| Boosters | Strengthen claims, show confidence | definitely, clearly, undoubtedly, confirm, demonstrate, prove, reveal, establish |
| Neutral reporting verbs | Present information objectively | state, report, find, note, describe, identify, examine, analyse, evaluate, summarise |

Use hedges and boosters in alignment with the actual strength of the evidence.

## Reflective Writing

### Models

| Model | Key idea |
|---|---|
| Dewey | A "problem condition" (a habit that does not work) becomes the springboard for reflective thought and deliberate change |
| Rodgers | Reflection as meaning-making, disciplined inquiry, social practice, and valuing growth |
| Kolb | Four-stage cycle: concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualisation, active experimentation |
| Critical reflection | Examine assumptions, evaluate their validity in real-life contexts, act to inform future practice |

### Descriptive vs Critical

- Descriptive: what happened, what was good or bad and why, likes and dislikes,
  what others did, how learning might be used.
- Critical: adds assumptions, validity, and future action.

### Structure And Vocabulary

A reflection typically follows three parts: **description** (short),
**interpretation** (most important), and **outcome**.

Key stems:

- Interpretation: "For me, the most significant aspect was...", "My learning
  arose from...", "I realised...", "This was because of..."
- Outcome: "Having analysed...", "I now think...", "I have learned that...",
  "As a next step, I need to..."

Tense rules: present tense for ideas, theories, and models; past tense for
events.

## Exam-Style Quick Questions

1. What are the three components of the Paul-Elder framework?
2. List the eight elements of thought.
3. Give one checking question for each element.
4. What is the difference between a positive and a negative feedback loop?
5. What are the three parts of a paragraph, and what is the function of each?
6. Define unity, coherence, and cohesion.
7. Give two hedge verbs and two booster verbs.
8. What are the four stages of Kolb's cycle?
9. How does a critical reflection differ from a descriptive reflection?
10. What three moves complete a critical reflection?
