# Weeks 10 and 11: Presentation Design and Delivery

## Overview

These sessions prepare the team presentation, the oral component of the
proposal, delivered in-class. The presentation content should be based on your
proposal, but it should not simply be an explanation of the proposal. Rather,
the presentation should be viewed as an opportunity to pitch the proposal to
the same stakeholders for whom it was written. The aim of the presentation is
to persuade the audience of the merits of the proposed solution and to
emphasize its clear benefits for their needs.

## Assessment Overview

- Weighting: 20%.
- Individual effectiveness (80%): content, verbal skills, non-verbal skills.
- Team performance (20%): team dynamics, visual aids.
- Each member should aim to speak for about 3 minutes.
- The full presentation, including Q&A, is limited to 20 minutes.
- A Q&A follows each presentation, and every team member should participate.
- Submit the slides as one PDF on the presentation day; only one team member
  submits the file to the LMS.

## Team Performance

### Team Dynamics

- Evidence of professionalism and teamwork, for example handing over, equal
  airtime, and respect and consideration among the team.
- Logical development of ideas across sections.
- Use of cohesive strategies and devices.

### Visual Aids

- Slide design: colours, font type and size, balance of text and graphics, and
  overall visual appeal.
- Language and references.
- Include a References slide as the team's final slide.
- Submit the slides as a single PDF on the assessment day through one team
  member.

## Preparation Tips

1. **Turn off the computer.** The best presenters sketch out their ideas and
   objectives with a pen and paper first. Going analog and sketching out your
   ideas, creating a rough storyboard, can help solidify and simplify messages
   in your head and give you a clear visual image of how the content will flow.
2. **Prepare with the end in mind.** Ask what the real purpose of your talk is,
   why you were asked to speak, what the audience expects, and what the most
   important takeaways are. Content is king: start with solid content
   appropriate for your audience, then build it into a winning story that you
   use to connect with your audience.
3. **Know your audience as much as possible.** Consider who the audience is,
   their backgrounds, how much background information they bring, what is in it
   for them, and what their pain is that you can help with. Consider the
   purpose of the event, the venue and logistics, the time of day, and the
   order of presenters.
4. **Keep it simple.** Do not fall into the trap of thinking that in order for
   your audience to understand anything you must tell them everything. Decide
   what to include and exclude. Identify the core idea, the fundamental
   takeaway, in a sentence or two, and add at most one or two other key
   takeaways.
5. **Ask yourself "so what?".** Check whether each point is relevant and
   important to your story. If you cannot really answer that question, cut that
   bit of content out of your talk.
6. **Prepare with the 10-minute rule in mind.** Studies support the idea that
   we tend to lose attention after 9-10 minutes in meetings, presentations, or
   classes. Design changes of pace into your talk: use short videos, a variety
   of charts and graphs, examples and stories, large easy-to-see photos or
   diagrams, and questions or a short quiz.
7. **Develop a simple structure.** The audience should never feel lost or
   wonder where you are going. Many presentations follow a problem/solution
   structure: the first part introduces the problem with background information
   and supporting data, examples, stories, and evidence; the second part shifts
   to ideas for dealing with the issue. Use design cues such as section title
   slides of a different colour to signal when a new section begins.
8. **Use stories and examples to illustrate your point.** The more complicated
   or difficult your point, the more important it is to use an example, a
   hypothetical, or a true story. Audiences remember the stories and the
   visualizations associated with the data, not just the data.
9. **Prepare a proper handout.** Create a handout or leave-behind that
   summarizes your main points or goes deeper. It should not just be a printout
   of your slides. Some slides, such as charts, graphs, and important photos,
   can be included in the document. A proper handout frees you from having to
   put loads of text on a screen.
10. **Work on your opening and ending.** The audience is likely to remember the
    beginning and the ending the most. Grab attention from the beginning with a
    surprising statistic or a short, compelling story. Do not end your talk
    simply with Q&A; after the last question, share a relevant story or short
    video clip to reinforce your key message, and make your call to action
    explicit.
11. **Have a backup plan.** Keep your data and slides on a thumb drive. Plan
    for the worst case, such as a broken projector or non-working monitors. If
    things go really bad, have a plan, such as using a whiteboard to share the
    key highlights, so you can engage with your audience without delay.

## Design Tips

1. **Keep it simple.** Your slides should have plenty of empty space (white or
   negative space). Do not feel compelled to fill empty areas with logos or
   unnecessary graphics. The less clutter, the more powerful the visual
   message.
2. **Make it visual.** According to the picture superiority effect, pictures
   are remembered better than words, especially when people are casually
   exposed to the information for a very limited time.
3. **Do not create "slideuments".** Slides are slides and documents are
   documents. Attempts to merge them result in a "slideument" (slide +
   document).
4. **Aim for a high signal-to-noise ratio.** The signal-to-noise ratio is the
   ratio of relevant to irrelevant information in a display.
5. **Design for the back of the room.** Make sure the content is readable from
   the back.
6. **Make images large, often fullscreen.** Generally, try to design your slide
   so it features an image fullscreen, then place your text on top in an area
   with good empty space.
7. **Use the principle of contrast** to highlight and make visuals clear.
   Achieve contrast through the manipulation of space, through colour choices
   (dark and light, cool and warm), typeface selection, and the positioning of
   elements.
8. **Use the principle of the rule of thirds** to create balance and interest.
   Divide the slide by real or imagined lines into four intersecting lines and
   nine boxes. Place your main subject at these crossing points rather than in
   the centre.
9. **Use clear charts and graphs.** Restrain: stop adding more, as most
   presenters include too much information in a display. Reduce: remove the
   nonessential. Emphasize: make the most important or salient point clear to
   the audience.
10. **Choose your fonts well.** Sans serif is often the preferred typeface
    style. Create type that is large enough to be easily understood in an
    instant.
11. **Use short video clips.** A video clip illustrates your point better and
    serves as a change of pace, increasing the interest of your audience.
12. **Use colours with purpose, and choose just a few.** Cool colours (such as
    blue and green) work best for backgrounds, as they appear to recede into
    the background. Warm colours (such as orange and red) generally work best
    for foreground objects such as text, as they appear to come at us. In a
    dark room, a dark background with white or light text works fine, but if
    you keep most of the lights on, a white background with black or dark text
    works much better.
13. **Limit the use of animation and transition effects.** Movement is the
    thing the brain notices most. Use object builds, effects, and slide
    transitions judiciously.
14. **Spend time in the slide sorter.** In the slide sorter view you can see
    how the logical flow of your presentation is progressing, and decide
    whether to break one slide into two or three for a more natural and logical
    flow.

## Delivery Tips

1. **Start strong.** First impressions are powerful. Open with a key and
   surpassing data visualization or a very short story that grabs attention and
   illustrates part of your key message. Early on, let the audience know what
   is in it for them and why they should care.
2. **Show your passion.** You are curious about your topic and you think it
   matters. Your feelings are contagious. Enthusiasm, passion, and genuine
   interest will be felt by the audience.
3. **Keep it short.** Humans have short attention spans when it comes to
   passively listening to a speaker. Attention is greatest at the opening and
   again when you say something like "In conclusion...". Finish earlier than
   expected. It is better to have the audience wanting more than to feel they
   have had more than enough.
4. **Move away from the lectern.** Get closer to your audience by moving away
   from or in front of the lectern or podium. The lectern is a barrier between
   you and the audience, but the goal of your presentation is to connect with
   the audience.
5. **Use a remote-control device.** A handheld remote will allow you to move
   away from the lectern.
6. **Remember the "B" key.** Pressing "B" while the slides are showing blanks
   the screen to black (pressing "W" makes it white). With the slide blank, all
   the attention can be placed back on you. Press the key again and the image
   reappears.
7. **Make good eye contact.** Try looking at individuals rather than scanning
   the group. Never turn your back on the audience. While maintaining eye
   contact, smile. If you can, record yourself presenting, as you may find you
   looked more grumpy or low-energy than you realized, and sometimes you have to
   amp up your energy.
8. **Keep the lights on.** Turning the lights off induces sleep and puts all
   the focus on the screen. The audience should be looking at you more than the
   screen. Today's projectors are bright enough to allow you to keep many or
   all of the lights on.
9. **At all times be courteous, gracious, and professional.** When audience
   members ask questions or give comments, thank them for their input. Even if
   someone is being difficult, keep to the high ground and deal with them
   courteously. The true professional can always remain cool and in control.
10. **Dress the part.** Generally, dress a little more formally than the
    audience. It is better to be too formal than too casual, since you can
    always remove a tie or jacket, but it is nearly impossible to increase the
    formality of your attire if you did not bring the clothes.
11. **Deal with nervousness.** The more on top of your material you are, the
    less nervous you will be. Know the logical flow, design professional and
    appropriate supporting materials, and rehearse several times. We fear what
    we do not know; when you remove the unknown, confidence will grow.
12. **Meet the audience.** Arrive early to make sure everything is set and
    working. Before your talk, mingle and talk to a few people, and ask why
    they are interested in the topic or what questions they have. This reduces
    your nerves and makes the audience familiar rather than an abstract blob.

## Non-Verbal Cues

Use non-verbal cues to convey meaning and create interest in your talk.
Consider these skills:

- vocal qualities;
- eye contact;
- facial expression;
- body language;
- physical appearance;
- space and proximity.

## Crafting An Effective Introduction

An effective introduction:

- gains attention and interest, and sets the tone of the talk;
- introduces the topic, clearly stating the topic;
- relates the topic to the audience, showing relevance and motivating the
  audience to listen;
- establishes the speaker's credibility, giving the audience a reason to pay
  attention;
- announces the specific purpose statement, indicating the thesis of the talk
  and what the audience can expect to gain;
- previews the body of the speech, providing a bridge to the first main point.

Attention-getting openings include: a reference to the subject or occasion, a
personal reference or narrative, a rhetorical question, a startling statement,
the use of humor, a quotation, or a statistic.

## Crafting An Effective Conclusion

An effective conclusion:

- reviews the main points;
- reminds the audience of the importance or significance of what has been said
  about the topic;
- asks the audience to take appropriate action;
- ends with a memorable thought;
- always thanks the audience at the end of the talk.

## Delivering The Message

- Project your voice.
- Articulate your words clearly.
- Be mindful of your speech pace.

### Sounding Professional And Confident

- Be concise and avoid wordiness. Remove words and phrases that do not add
  meaning to your sentence. For example, use "because" instead of "due to the
  fact that", "if" instead of "in the event that", and "to be" instead of "for
  the purpose of being".
- Avoid over-using filler words and a casual way of speaking, such as "I'm kind
  of like hoping you will like our presentation" or "you know".
- Avoid words used out of habit that do not add value or information, such as
  "actually", "basically", "and then", and "so".
- Use language clearly: familiar words and concrete words.
- Use language correctly: note grammatical accuracy.
- Use language appropriately: to the occasion, the audience, and the topic.

### Signposting

Use signposting to help the audience stay focused and attentive and to navigate
through your presentation, for example:

- "Now that we've discussed ..., I'd like to talk about ..."
- "Let me now give you details about ... To be precise, ..."
- "In contrast, ..."
- "Next, ..."

## Dealing With Questions

- Ask for clarification if you do not understand the question: "I'm afraid I
  don't quite understand your question."
- Politely avoid answering a question that is irrelevant: "If you don't mind,
  I'd prefer not to discuss that today."
- Admit when you do not know the answer: "I'm afraid I don't know the answer to
  your question, but I'll try to find out for you."
- Rephrase aggressive questions. For example, "Do you honestly believe we can
  get the contract?" becomes "If I understand you correctly, you want to know
  if it is possible to get the contract."
- Rephrase negative questions. For example, "Isn't there a better solution?"
  becomes "I see. So what you're asking is: what would a better solution be?"

## Preparing The Team Presentation

Discuss these questions with your project group members:

- Who is your target audience?
- What is the purpose of your presentation?
- What information, primary and secondary, is relevant to the talk?
- How can your audience most effectively be convinced of the merits of your
  message?
- What key takeaways do you want to leave with your audience?
- What information will each member of your group be presenting?
- What happens when you cannot answer a question from the audience?
- How will every member prepare to participate actively in the Q&A?

## Rehearsing The Presentation

Find a time to rehearse together. Consider:

- about 3 minutes per member and 20 minutes in total, including Q&A;
- the use of visuals;
- audience engagement strategies;
- possible questions from the audience;
- possible responses to questions;
- ways of handling difficult members of the audience.

Consider rehearsing with a presentation speaker coach feature if it is
available in your presentation software.
