# Week 9: Executive Summary and Front-Back Matter

## Overview

This session covers the conclusion, the front matter (cover page, table of
contents, and executive summary), and the back matter (references and
appendices) of the proposal.

## The Conclusion

The conclusion is a key element of any academic text:

- It sums up your main argument as well as the key points articulated in each
  main (sub-)section.
- It clearly relays the writer's key take-home message.
- End the conclusion with a sentence that conveys your final point clearly and
  succinctly, leaving the reader with a strong impression of its significance
  within a broader context.

Caution: avoid using your conclusion to introduce new information or new ideas,
because this can confuse the reader.

Sample pattern: a conclusion on recycling restates the key benefits (conserving
resources, lowering the need to collect raw materials, keeping usable materials
out of landfills, reducing the carbon footprint, and lowering pollutants),
connects them to a broader context (combating climate change), and ends by
emphasizing that recycling is an important part of the much-needed effort.

## Sections Of A Proposal

The full proposal is made up of:

- front matter: the cover page, the table of contents, and the executive
  summary;
- the main text: Introduction, Literature Review, Body, Methods, and
  Conclusion;
- back matter: references and appendices.

## Front Matter

### Cover Page

The cover page should include the following:

- the institution's logo;
- the formal name of the assignment (as stated in the assignment brief);
- the name of your proposal (as created by your team);
- the names of the team members, each with the format Name and matriculation
  number;
- the date of completion of the proposal.

### Table Of Contents

The table of contents should list the executive summary, the main content, and
the back matter, including the reference page. A good table of contents should
be easy to read and accurately formatted.

### Executive Summary

What it is:

- Also known as the opening statement.
- Gives a brief overview of what the proposal offers and why this proposal
  should be accepted by a client.
- Provides a clear idea of what the proposal seeks to accomplish, without too
  many technical details.
- Suggested length: around 200 words.
- Recommendation: write the executive summary last, after completing the rest
  of the proposal.

Parts of an executive summary:

1. **The opening.** Its purpose is to get the client's attention. It can
   include an interesting story, a surprising fact, or an insightful quote (a
   hook). Focus on the issue and the potential result, and be direct, concise,
   and evocative. This is where you get the client interested and even excited
   about what you are about to propose.
2. **Establish the need / state the problem.** Demonstrate your understanding
   of the issue, that is, your grasp of the situation or problem. Include your
   research on the issue. Explain the problem, what is not working effectively.
   State how your client will benefit from solving the problem, what can
   change, and the potential positive outcomes.
3. **Proposed solution.** Briefly state the solution your team is proposing,
   but only as an overview, because the details will follow in the proposal.
4. **A short conclusion.** Sum up the benefits of your proposal. Explain why
   your client would benefit from doing the work you recommend, and how your
   recommendation(s) will improve the existing situation. Create a call to
   action and connect with the goals of your reader.

Illustrations of effective wording:

- An opening that states the problem concretely ("We are experiencing a $10
  million shortfall in yearly revenue due to telecommunications network
  outages") reads better than a dry description of what the document contains.
- A proposed solution that is concrete ("Purchase and install a pilot system.
  This will allow us to test the new software without endangering our
  day-to-day operations") reads better than vague, jargon-heavy phrasing.
- A specific request ("we need you to approve an increase of $2 million in next
  quarter's IT budget") reads better than a general statement of concern.

Dos and don'ts: do create a sense of urgency in the implications,
recommendations, and conclusions presented in the executive summary.

## Back Matter

### References

- Follow APA 7th edition.
- Caution: check carefully to make sure you are not using the 6th edition
  rules, especially for books and online resources.

### Appendices

- Appendices contain additional details or relevant information referred to or
  identified in the proposal.
- They include graphs, tables, survey results, and similar material.
- Appendices should be labelled (for example, Appendix A, Appendix B).
- They are not included in the word count.

## Word Count Note

Good news: the executive summary, references, and appendices are not part of
the word count. Bad news: they will still be assessed as part of the written
proposal.

## Proofreading

Remember to proofread your proposal: check for coherence and cohesion, correct
grammar, and clear sentence structure, and follow the formatting instructions
given in the brief.
