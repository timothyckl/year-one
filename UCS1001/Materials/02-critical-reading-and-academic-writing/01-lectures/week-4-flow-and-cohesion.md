# Week 4: Flow and Cohesion

## Overview

Effective paragraphs need flow: a smooth and logical progression of ideas. Three
key elements produce an effective paragraph: unity, coherence, and cohesion.

## Three key elements of an effective paragraph

| Element | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Unity** | One idea from start to finish. The function of each sentence is to illustrate, clarify, or explain; all sentences connect to the main idea, and only relevant information is included. |
| **Coherence** | Logical order of sentences/information. There is a connection between the last sentence and the topic sentence that starts the paragraph. |
| **Cohesion** | Smooth and logical connection of ideas. There is a clear relationship between sentences, supported by the use of cohesive devices. |

## Achieving cohesion: strategies

### 1. Repeated words / ideas

Repeating key words and ideas ties sentences together and keeps the reader
focused on the central concept.

Example (on the neglected art of thinking): the words "think" and "thinking"
are repeated throughout the passage - from "we make no room for thought in our
daily lives" to "some time to think", "people who think too much", and
"almost anything is more important than thinking".

### 2. Reference words (it / this / these / which)

Pronouns and determiners refer back to an idea already introduced, so the
reader knows the sentences are connected.

Example: "Cohesion is an important feature of academic writing. **It** can help
ensure that your writing coheres or 'sticks together', **which** will make it
easier for the reader to follow the main ideas in your essay or report. You can
achieve good cohesion by paying attention to five important features. The first
of **these** is repeated words. The second key feature is reference words. The
third one is transition signals. The fourth is substitution. The final
important aspect is ellipsis."

### 3. Transitional signals (cohesive devices / linking words)

Transition words and phrases show the relationship between ideas.

Paragraph without transitions:

> Drawing is all about taking the time to master each step. Mastering the art
> of drawing doesn't happen in a day. It's a skill that takes daily practice.
> You need dedication to hone your craft. It's important not to burn yourself
> out. You need to stick with it.

Paragraph with transitions:

> Drawing is all about taking the time to master each step. **In other words**,
> mastering the art of drawing doesn't happen in a day. **Furthermore**, it's a
> skill that takes daily practice **as well as** dedication to hone your craft.
> It's important not to burn yourself out, **but** you need to stick with it.

### 4. Substitution (using synonyms)

Replacing a word with another word that means the same thing avoids repetition
while keeping the same idea in view.

Example (on energy sources): "Energy is the most important resource of modern
society: without **power** ... humans were confident that the world's supply of
**fossil fuel** would last forever. ... our supplies of **coal, gas and oil**
are destined to run out. The production of **nuclear energy** ... investigating
alternative sources of energy and, especially that collected from the sun.
Indeed, **solar power** seems the most promising for now."

## Case study: Mercury, Gemini, Apollo

Two paragraphs describe the U.S. manned space exploration projects. Both contain
the same facts, but they differ in flow.

Paragraph 1 uses plain sentences, e.g. "Whether or not human beings could
survive and function in outer space was important to study in the Mercury
project."

Paragraph 2 improves flow using cohesive strategies, e.g. "For example, the
Mercury project was designed to test whether or not human beings could survive
and function in outer space."

Key improvements in paragraph 2:

- **Transitions** introduce examples and additions: "for example", "in
  addition", "as another example", "finally".
- **Parallel structure** across the project sentences: "The Mercury project was
  designed to ...", "The Gemini project was intended to ...", "The Apollo
  project had the goal of ...".
- **Summary words** refer back to an idea: "this procedure", "this linking".
- **Known information before new information**: the project is named first,
  then its specific goals are introduced.

## Strategies to develop continuity

1. **Repeat/rephrase key words** - e.g. project, goal, test.
2. **Place known information before new information** - e.g. mention the
   project first before introducing the specific goals of a project.
3. **Use transitions/logical connectors** - e.g. for example, in addition, as
   another example, finally.
4. **Use parallel structure** - e.g.
   - The Mercury project was designed to ...
   - The Gemini project was intended to ...
   - The Apollo project had the goal of ...
5. **Use a summary word** - this/these + a summary noun that refers back to the
   idea in the previous sentence, e.g. this procedure, this linking.
