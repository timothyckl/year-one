# Week 4: Paraphrasing

## Overview

Writing for academic and technical purposes often involves conducting secondary
research (i.e. reading informed, credible sources) and incorporating the
findings into your own work through paraphrasing others' ideas.

In a paraphrase, a writer simply puts another writer's ideas into their own
words.

## Paraphrasing strategies

After reading a short text (a clause, a sentence, or several sentences), to
write a paraphrase you should:

- highlight the main points;
- use different vocabulary while maintaining the original meaning;
- change the sentence structures;
- change the order of major ideas, where possible;
- use synonyms that are appropriate for academic or technical writing (for
  example, avoid phrasal verbs such as "point out" when a one-word equivalent,
  such as "explain", can be used);
- compare the two versions to ensure that enough rewording has taken place BUT
  the meaning is the same;
- credit the original author(s) using the appropriate style (i.e. the APA 7th
  edition style).

## Worked example

**Original text:** "Most people who work closely with animals, such as animal
trainers, take it as a matter of fact that animals have emotions."

Source: Masson, J., & McCarthy, S. (1995). *When elephants weep: The emotional
lives of animals*. Dell Publishing.

**Paraphrase:** Masson and McCarthy (1995) state that almost all people who
have interacted intimately with animals know that they have the capacity to
feel.

Note how the paraphrase: changes the vocabulary ("most people who work closely
with" becomes "almost all people who have interacted intimately with"),
restructures the sentence, and credits the authors with an in-text citation.

## Practice examples

**Example 2 - paraphrase it yourself.**

Original text: "The current approach to telematics is generally to install a
device in a customer's car for six months and have it relay the driver's
actions back to the insurer for evaluation."

Source: Carroll, P. (2015, September 29). The problem with telematics.
Insurance Thought Leadership.com.

One common transformation here is moving from an active sentence to a passive
sentence.

**Example 3 - paraphrase it yourself.**

Original text: "By permanently and remotely monitoring the status of key
elements of the train and infrastructure, maintenance interventions can be
planned upon the detection of warning information before a fault occurs."

Source: *The connected journey*. (2016, June 3). *Rail Engineer*.

## Be careful: don't change the meaning

When you paraphrase, make sure you don't change the meaning of the original
statement.

- "The woman played the piano with the curved legs" should NOT be paraphrased
  as "The woman with curved legs played the piano".

The original says the piano has the curved legs, not the woman. Rephrasing must
preserve the relationships between the ideas in the original.

## Citing when you paraphrase

When you paraphrase information from a source, you must cite the source. This
includes both in-text and end-of-text citations (i.e. references).

## Three common types of in-text citation (APA 7th edition)

**Type #1:** surname + (year of publication) + reporting verb + paraphrased
text.

> Moisse (2010) states that children conceived via assisted reproductive
> technology are more likely to be plagued with obesity, hypertension and Type
> 2 diabetes.

**Type #2:** preposition + surname + (year of publication) + paraphrased text.

> According to Moisse (2010), children conceived via assisted reproductive
> technology are more likely to be plagued with obesity, hypertension and Type
> 2 diabetes.

**Type #3:** paraphrased text + (surname + year of publication).

> Children conceived via assisted reproductive technology are more likely to
> be plagued with obesity, hypertension and Type 2 diabetes later in life
> (Moisse, 2010).
