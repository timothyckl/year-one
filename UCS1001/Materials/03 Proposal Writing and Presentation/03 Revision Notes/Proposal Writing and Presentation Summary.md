# Proposal Writing and Presentation Summary

Quick-reference revision covering Weeks 6, 8, 9, and 10-11. For detailed notes,
see the individual lecture pages.

## Week 6: Assignments And Preparing The Technical Proposal

- Assignment 3 is a group research proposal of not more than 2000 words
  (Introduction through Conclusion) in teams of 4-5. Assignment 4 is the team oral
  presentation of the proposal.
- Freeloading practice: the default is 100% of the assessment score per group
  member; the score of a non-contributing member can be reduced by group
  agreement, communicated to the tutor upon submission.
- A proposal is a persuasive document: it defines what a project is about,
  explains why it is important, and convinces the reader of a well thought-
  through technical approach.
- Proposal information elements (DMAIC): problem statement, root causes,
  objectives including anticipated benefits, solutions considered, approach
  and novelty, arguments, technical approach / methodology, and conclusion.
- Problem statement (the "why"): current situation; limitations and root
  causes. Sources: primary analysis tools (gap analysis, fishbone, SWOT, 5
  Whys) and the secondary literature review.
- Objectives (the "what"): align with the problem statement; clear, specific,
  and measurable.
- Proposed improvements: based on the analysis of the problem; feasible;
  consider stakeholders and market solutions; best presented graphically.
- Critical thinking approach: identify the key concept and problem, analyze
  the problem from many angles, brainstorm several solutions, then decide
  which solution fits best.

## Week 8: Introduction And Literature Review

### Introduction

- Purpose: provide background information, establish the significance of the
  study, and reveal the objective.
- Components: background paragraph (PEEL framework), problem statement
  paragraph (context and relevance), purpose statement, and scope.
- Purpose statement: flows from the problem statement; specific and precise;
  concise (max two sentences); clear; goal-oriented.
- Scope: what will be discussed (and reasonably what will not); needs and
  desired outcomes; limitations; plus tasks, timeline, resources, risks, and
  deliverables.

### Literature Review

- Review paragraphs follow a claim + evidence structure.
- Review strategies: contrast interpretations; connect source texts; connect
  the literature to the current study; point out limitations and potential;
  note the scope of previous research and the niche of the current study;
  point out weaknesses.
- Fallacies to avoid: faulty conclusions from skeletal evidence; presenting
  conclusions without addressing alternatives; appeals to emotion; appeals to
  ignorance; misplaced causality; begging the question; disconnected
  conclusions without evidence.
- Author's voice: show critical interpretation and evaluation of the
  literature.
- Language: use the active voice, precise verbs, concrete terms, and positive
  wording; avoid jargon, statements that cannot be backed up, cliches, and
  buzzwords.

## Week 9: Body, Conclusion, And Front-Back Matter

- Body: proposed improvement and rationale; specific objectives and benefits;
  sustainability across planet, people, profit, and principles; comparison with
  the current system; and adaptations for future developments or disruptions.
- Methods: justified methods for evaluating the efficiency and sustainability
  of the improved system, including possible unintended consequences, costs,
  and varied stakeholder perspectives.
- Conclusion: sums up the main argument and the key points of each main
  section; relays the take-home message; ends with a strong final impression;
  do not introduce new information or ideas.
- Front matter: cover page (institution logo, formal assignment name, proposal
  name, team names with matriculation numbers, date of completion), table of
  contents, and executive summary.
- Executive summary: write it last; around 200 words; opening (hook), establish
  the need / problem, proposed solution overview, and a short conclusion with a
  call to action.
- Back matter: references in APA 7th edition and labelled appendices
  (not in the word count).
- Word count: the executive summary, references, and appendices are excluded
  from the word count but are still assessed.
- Proofread for coherence, cohesion, correct grammar, clear sentence
  structure, and formatting per the brief.

## Weeks 10 and 11: Presentation Design And Delivery

- Assignment 4 breakdown: individual effectiveness (80%) covering content,
  verbal skills, and non-verbal skills; team performance (20%) covering team
  dynamics and visual aids.
- Timing and submission: about 3 minutes per member and 20 minutes in total,
  including Q&A; all members participate in Q&A; one member submits the slides
  as a single PDF on the presentation day.
- Preparation: sketch first; prepare with the end in mind; know the audience;
  keep it simple; ask "so what?"; design around the 10-minute attention rule;
  develop a simple structure; use stories and examples; prepare a proper
  handout; work on the opening and ending; have a backup plan.
- Design: keep it simple (white space); make it visual; no "slideuments"; aim
  for a high signal-to-noise ratio; design for the back of the room; make
  images large; use contrast; use the rule of thirds; use clear charts and
  graphs; choose fonts well; use short video clips; use colours with purpose;
  limit animation and transitions; spend time in the slide sorter.
- Delivery: start strong; show passion; keep it short; move away from the
  lectern; use a remote-control device; remember the "B" key; make good eye
  contact; keep the lights on; be courteous, gracious, and professional; dress
  the part; deal with nervousness; meet the audience.
- Structure: the introduction gains attention, introduces the topic, relates it
  to the audience, establishes credibility, announces the purpose statement,
  and previews the body; the conclusion reviews main points, reminds of
  significance, asks for action, ends memorably, and thanks the audience.
- Language: be concise; avoid filler words and casual speech; use clear,
  correct, and appropriate language; use signposting.
- Questions: ask for clarification, politely avoid irrelevant questions, admit
  when you do not know, and rephrase aggressive or negative questions.
- Rehearse together: time limit, visuals, engagement strategies, possible
  questions and responses, and handling difficult audience members.
