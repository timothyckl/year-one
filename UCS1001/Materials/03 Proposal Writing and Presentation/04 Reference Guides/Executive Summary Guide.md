# Executive Summary Guide

## What It Is

The executive summary, also known as the opening statement, is part of the
front matter of the proposal. It:

- gives a brief overview of what the proposal offers and why this proposal
  should be accepted by a client;
- provides a clear idea of what the proposal seeks to accomplish, without too
  many technical details.

## When To Write It

Write the executive summary last, after completing the rest of the proposal.
This lets you summarize and persuade rather than develop new arguments.

## Length

Around 200 words.

## Structure

1. **The opening.** Get the client's attention. It can include an interesting
   story, a surprising fact, or an insightful quote (a hook). Focus on the
   issue and the potential result, and be direct, concise, and evocative.
2. **Establish the need / state the problem.** Demonstrate your understanding
   of the issue. Include your research on the issue. Explain what is not
   working effectively. State how the client will benefit from solving the
   problem, what can change, and the potential positive outcomes.
3. **Proposed solution.** Briefly state the solution your team is proposing,
   but only as an overview, since the details will follow in the proposal.
4. **A short conclusion.** Sum up the benefits of the proposal. Explain why the
   client would benefit from doing the recommended work and how the
   recommendation improves the existing situation. Create a call to action and
   connect with the goals of the reader.

## Dos And Don'ts

- Do create a sense of urgency in the implications, recommendations, and
  conclusions presented in the executive summary.
- Do be specific and concrete. For example, state the problem concretely ("We
  are experiencing a $10 million shortfall in yearly revenue due to
  telecommunications network outages") rather than dryly describing what the
  document contains.
- Do present the solution concretely, such as proposing to purchase and install
  a pilot system to test new software without endangering day-to-day
  operations.
- Do make a specific request, such as an approval to increase a budget by a
  stated amount, rather than a general statement of concern.
- Do not use vague, jargon-heavy language that obscures the message.

## Related Back Matter

- References: follow APA 7th edition. Check carefully that you are not using
  6th edition rules, especially for books and online resources.
- Appendices: contain additional details or relevant information referred to in
  the proposal (graphs, tables, survey results, and similar material). Label
  them (for example, Appendix A, Appendix B). They are not included in the word
  count.
- Word count: the executive summary, references, and appendices are excluded
  from the word count, but they are still assessed as part of the written
  proposal.
