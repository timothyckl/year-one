# Week 8: Writing the Literature Review

## Overview

The literature review is the second section of the proposal. It summarizes,
synthesizes, and critically evaluates the past research that is relevant to the
problem and to the proposed solution, establishing what is known, what is
missing, and where the current study fits.

## The "Review" In A Literature Review

The review happens at the paragraph level. The review paragraph usually follows
the claim (the point you are trying to prove) plus evidence (the proof)
structure.

### Common Review Strategies

The common strategies used to write a critical literature review are:

- contrasting two different interpretations;
- showing the connections between different source texts;
- showing the connections between the literature and the current study;
- pointing out the limitations and potential of different theories;
- pointing out the scope of previous research and the niche of the current
  study;
- pointing out the weaknesses of a particular type of study.

### Examples Of The Strategies In Use

- **Showing the connection between the literature and the current study:**
  lengthy queues and bottlenecks tend to occur for both food-ordering queues
  and secondary food-service queues during peak periods, which may incline
  customers to leave their current queue for another stall. This behaviour can
  commonly be seen when queues become too long.
- **Showing the connections between different source texts:** various factors
  have been found to affect customer wait time and corresponding sales. One
  study found that a decrease in service time can increase sales capacity, and
  vice-versa. Another noted that businesses should manage the expectations of
  their customers and that queues should commensurate with the value of the
  food being sold, because uncertainty in waiting time may lead customers to
  negative emotions and to interpret long waits as poor management.
- **Contrasting two different interpretations, then pointing out the niche of
  the current study:** manpower shortages in the food and beverage industry are
  well noted, so careful consideration is needed to maintain customer
  satisfaction and waiting-time experience while ensuring business viability.
  Hence, more research is needed to modify the existing food-ordering system,
  possibly through an online mobile application that addresses manpower
  constraints while providing customers with the convenience of purchasing food
  without queuing.

## Identifying Review Strategies

Practise identifying the strategies used in a structured literature review. For
example, a review of wearable technology and physical activity can be organized
under themes:

- **Data accuracy:** the effectiveness of a fitness tracker can be influenced by
  how accurate the device is. Vital-data computation in distributed trackers is
  not always accurate and consistent, and sensor technologies are mostly
  identical between devices, so it is the algorithm rather than the technology
  that seems to primarily impact device accuracy. An efficient algorithm would
  produce more precise data, making the algorithm a relevant parameter to
  explore.
- **Personalisation of data:** interventions to increase physical activity are
  more effective when they are personalised. One study showed an improvement in
  activity level in several patients, but the sample size was too small to
  determine the effectiveness of the intervention, so employing the
  intervention in a larger sample is a relevant parameter to explore.
- **Incentives:** rewards-based physical activity programmes attract
  insufficiently active employees. Cash incentives were most effective at
  increasing activity at six months, but the effect was not sustained six
  months after the incentives were discontinued.

## Fallacies To Avoid

- Jumping to a faulty conclusion, based on skeletal evidence or incomplete
  evaluation of the evidence.
- Presenting a conclusion without properly addressing other alternatives (for
  example, stating that A caused B without considering any other possible cause
  of B).
- Appealing to emotions rather than evidence (for example, defending a process
  only because it was formalized by a previous head of department and has
  worked for the past ten years).
- Appealing to ignorance by assuming that a claim is true because it has not
  been proven false (for example, "It's obvious to all of us that ...").
- Misplaced causality, involving arguments from cause to effect or effect to
  cause (for example, concluding that teachers are incompetent from low test
  scores).
- Begging the question, which occurs when a claim is asserted and the same
  claim is used as evidence for the assertion (for example, "MacD sells the
  best fries. It's just so.").
- A disconnected conclusion without any evidence to support it (for example, an
  "everybody knows that" conclusion where none exists).

## Critical Evaluation: The Author's Voice

A review serves to reflect your critical interpretation and evaluation of past
research. The underlined phrases in a well-written review show interpretation
and evaluation, for example:

- noting that while sound reasons are provided for the eruption of a conflict,
  none of these arguments adequately engage the critical issue of entrenched
  ethnic tensions;
- pointing out the advantage of a work, why it is important for your purposes,
  and highlighting the systemic changes that underpin shifts in the phenomenon
  being studied;
- noting that clinical interpretations overlook the fact that mutuality and
  sociality make us human as much as extendable knowledge.

## Planning The Literature Review

In groups, identify the organization you will be using for your literature
review and write an outline for it. Use the Paul-Elder framework to help with
your proposal writing and to improve the quality of your work.

## Language Conventions For Writing A Proposal

- **Use the active voice wherever possible.** For example, "They found" (not
  "It was determined"), "The editors reviewed" (not "A review was done"), "We
  value your effort" (not "Your effort is valued"). The active voice makes sure
  there is no misunderstanding of who is doing what. An active sentence such as
  "Our team developed an online campaign that brought in 400 new monthly
  subscribers" reads better than the passive "An online campaign was developed
  that resulted in bringing in 400 new monthly subscribers."
- **Use precise verbs instead of abstract nouns.** For example, "extract, take"
  (not "engage in the extraction of"), "impact, affect" (not "have an impact
  on"), "start, help" (not "facilitate the implementation of"). Verbs express
  specific actions, so let them do their job. Abstract nouns are preferred in
  academic writing, not so much in technical writing.
- **Use concrete terms.** For example, "report, article" (not "instrument of
  communication"), "workers, employees" (not "human resources"), "we,
  researchers" (not "knowledge disseminators").
- **Use positive words instead of negative ones, as this is more persuasive.**
  For example, "possible" (not "not impossible"), "clear" (not "unlikely to be
  misinterpreted"), "can do X if Y" (not "unable to do X unless Y").
- **Do not use jargon or technical language** that your reader may not
  understand.
- **Do not make statements that cannot be backed up with evidence.**
- **Do not use cliches and buzzwords** such as "ground-breaking", "cutting
  edge", "world class", or "win-win".
