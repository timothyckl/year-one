## Group Project Freeloading Practice

At the programme level, the standard practice governing group project
freeloading is stated in the Student Handbook:

- It is the default position that every group member receives 100% of the
  assessment score for the group project.
- If a student does not contribute significantly, the rest of the group agrees
  on a percentage of the assessment score that the student should receive and
  communicates this to the tutor upon submission of the group project.
- The student is then awarded that percentage of the assessment score. For
  example, if the group agrees that a student should receive 50% of a group
  assessment score of 18 marks (out of 20), the student receives 9 marks (out
  of 20).

## Key Points For The Proposal

- Identify and elaborate on a specific system, design, process, procedure, or
  protocol that needs improvement (you could choose one suggested by the
  subject professor).
- Perform secondary research and write a literature review about the specific
  system, design, process, procedure, or protocol and the solutions currently
  available to improve it.
- Select one or a combination of existing solutions, or develop a solution, and
  explain the specific objectives you would like to achieve with the improved
  system, design, process, procedure, or protocol, and its contributions to
  sustainability compared with the current one.
- In the Methods section, elaborate on and justify the method(s) you propose to
  evaluate the efficiency and sustainability of the improved system, design,
  process, procedure, or protocol.

## Critical Thinking And Proposal Writing

Proposal writing is a critical-thinking and problem-solving exercise: you
identify a problem, analyse it, and propose a solution. Thinking critically not
only helps handle future challenges more skillfully, it also broadens
experience and helps gain perspective.

### A Four-Step Approach To A Team Proposal

1. **Identify the key concept.** Determine if a problem exists and exactly what
   it is.
2. **Analyze the problem - look at it from different angles.** Look at it from a
   variety of perspectives. Is it solvable? Is it real or perceived? What
   factors contribute to it? Looking at it from many angles can help you develop
   possible solutions, and you may discover a bias or narrow point of view that
   needs to be broadened.
3. **Brainstorm and develop several possible solutions.** Problems can be solved
   in many ways. Brainstorm a list of several possible solutions, then go over
   the list and narrow it down to the best possibilities. Having several viable
   options leads to obtaining the best results.
4. **Decide which solution fits the situation best.** Go over your list of
   possible solutions and determine what will work best for the problem at
   hand.

Keep the critical thinking framework in mind and use the Progress Record to
note down your discussions.

## Reading About The Topic

- Read as much as you can about your topic.
- Start with broad discussions about your topic (articles about the problem at
  an international or regional level), then move on to more local coverage.
- Some key sources are materials that describe how the problem is or was dealt
  with in other communities, regionally or globally. You can use this
  information as a comparison tool or to inform your solution.
