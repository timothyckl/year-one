# Week 4: Critical Reading and Synthesis

## Overview

Critical reading is active engagement with a text. It is a key part of critical
thinking and directly supports better academic writing. This unit covers the
four-stage critical reading process and synthesising information from multiple
sources.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

- understand the benefits of critical reading to academic writing;
- apply critical reading strategies to your own writing tasks;
- select relevant information from multiple sources and synthesise it.

## What is critical reading?

Critical reading is a deeper engagement with a text. It involves:

- **Asking questions** about the way the content is presented, for example:
  what is the author's argument or claim? What evidence does the author use to
  support this claim?
- **Evaluating** the claims and evidence presented by the writer, for example:
  is the argument logical or reasonable? What do other writers think about this
  topic? Is the evidence credible or acceptable? Is there contrary evidence?
- **Forming your own perspective** based on your enquiry, for example: after
  carefully evaluating the writer's claim and based on my own research, ...

Critical reading is active reading: you are not a passive receiver of content.

## The critical reading process

Four stages: setting a purpose, previewing, annotating, and evaluating.

### 1) Setting a purpose

- Helps to place the information in the text.
- Helps to determine whether the information is what you are looking for.

### 2) Previewing

- Gathers basic information before reading the text carefully.
- Involves asking questions such as:
  - Who is the author?
  - What information can I expect based on the title and visuals?
  - How is the text structured (compare and contrast; a description; a
    literature review)?

### 3) Annotating

- Taking notes on the text content:
  - the thesis and main points;
  - important ideas and the connections between them;
  - key (new or unfamiliar) terms and vocabulary;
  - points you want to research further;
  - confusing areas that need re-reading.

### 4) Evaluating

- Outline and summarise what the writer is discussing or asserting.
- Examine the relevance or validity of the author's claims by looking at:
  - the evidence and sources of information used by the author;
  - the assumptions or biases the author seems to show.

## Synthesis: incorporating sources of information

### Definition

Combining information from various sources, in a coherent and logical way, to
support your point in an essay or report.

- **A good synthesis** is a dialogue between information from different sources,
  and a dialogue between information from other sources and your own ideas.
- **What you synthesise:** relevant information from reliable journal articles,
  credible online sites, and so on.

### Steps for synthesising sources

1. Take relevant points from different sources.
2. Evaluate them.
3. Summarise them.
4. Plan how you want to synthesise (i.e. the order in which the points should be
   placed for coherence).
5. Combine them in a paragraph to support your argument.

### Summary vs synthesis

- A writer who only summarises each source found on the topic is not
  synthesising.
- A writer synthesises when they combine the sources, analyse their main ideas,
  and show how they contrast.

## Source integration

When you synthesise information into a paragraph, don't forget to document the
sources. There are two ways to do this:

- in-text citation;
- end-of-text citation (i.e. the reference list).

### In-text citation: three options

**Idea-focused:** place the author and date in brackets in an appropriate place
or at the end of the sentence.

> Researchers have studied how ... (Alibali et al., 2009; Siegler, 1976).
>
> A key benefit of sustainability is ... (Smith, 2018).

**Author-focused:** the writer's name is mentioned in the sentence and only the
date is placed in brackets.

> Chan (2019) states that ...
>
> According to Anson and Smith (2013), ...

**Chronology-focused:** integrate both the author and date into your sentence.

> In 2019, Anderson proposed a new concept ...

### End-of-text citation (reference list)

- The reference list should contain all the sources you used to write your
  report.
- It should be placed at the end of your text.
- The following common elements should appear in the list:
  - Author - there may be one or more authors, and all their names must be
    included;
  - date of publication - include dates for journal articles too;
  - title - include the full title of the work;
  - source - include the name of the publisher and/or web address (URL).

## Recap

- Critical reading is a key part of critical thinking.
- It is active engagement with a text and helps you produce better
  reports/essays.
- The four stages are: setting a purpose, previewing, annotating, and
  evaluating.
- Synthesising combines relevant information from different sources into a
  paragraph.
- Synthesis allows you to present different perspectives on a topic and to
  extend the discussion with your own view or conclusion.
