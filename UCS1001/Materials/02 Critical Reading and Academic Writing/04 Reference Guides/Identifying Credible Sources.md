# Identifying Credible Sources

Guidance on finding and evaluating credible sources for your academic work,
based on the library skills workshop.

## Learning outcomes

1. Evaluate and adapt search strategies to improve the relevance and quality of
   search results.
2. Evaluate the credibility and appropriateness of information sources based on
   context and intended use.

## Search strategies

Example task: find at least 3 academic journal articles on a topic such as
social media and greenwashing. AI tools can help generate a search strategy,
but human judgement is crucial, and a strong search strategy is essential for
finding relevant results.

### Working with a suggested search string

A machine-generated string may look thorough but can be too long. Useful
checks:

- **Use better synonyms** for key concepts (e.g. for greenwashing: "green
  washing", "green labelling", "green sheen").
- **Cut down drastically on the length of the string** - shorter strings
  usually search better.
- **Be careful with truncation.** Not all databases or search engines recognise
  truncation symbols; prefer to omit truncation for the first attempt.
- **Use phrase searching deliberately.** Phrase searching implies an exact
  phrase and not a single word (e.g. "debunk*" as a phrase will not search the
  word "debunking" the way truncation would).
- **Use Booleans correctly.** The Boolean operators (AND, OR) are a strength of
  many generated strings - verify them and keep them.

### A better strategy

A shorter, focused string with good synonyms and correct Booleans:

> (greenwashing OR "green washing" OR "green labelling" OR greenlabelling OR
> "green sheen") AND ("social media" OR "social networking")

### Learning points

- AI tools can help, but human judgement is crucial.
- Strong search strategies are essential for finding relevant results.

## Matching sources to the deliverable

Different deliverables have different audiences, purposes, and styles. Choose
and evaluate sources with the deliverable in mind.

| Deliverable | Audience | Purpose | Style |
|---|---|---|---|
| Technical report | University academics (experts) | Provide in-depth, evidence-based analysis | Formal, detailed, data-driven, uses technical language |
| Concept brief | Company decision-makers (non-specialists) | Present a practical, engaging solution concept | Concise, persuasive, easy to read, focuses on benefits |

## Evaluating sources: relevance, authority, purpose, context

Evaluate each source against criteria such as relevance, authority, purpose,
and context. A quick preliminary evaluation can be done with a CRAAP test.

### Sample evaluation - journal article (Source A)

| Criteria | Technical Report | Concept Brief |
|---|---|---|
| Relevance | Directly addresses the topic with empirical/theoretical depth; strong fit for academic analysis. | Provides depth but may be too technical; less usable for a practitioner audience. |
| Authority | Peer-reviewed, authored by scholars, published in a journal. | Authoritative, but academic framing may be less accessible for business leaders. |
| Purpose | To inform and advance knowledge; objective and evidence-based. | To inform and advance knowledge, but not written with practitioner needs in mind. |
| Context | Supports evidence, theory, and critical discussion for the technical report. | Lends credibility, but risks overwhelming a concept brief. |

### Sample evaluation - blog article (Source B)

| Criteria | Technical Report | Concept Brief |
|---|---|---|
| Relevance | Useful as supplementary context showing industry perspectives; not rigorous enough as primary evidence. | Offers practical strategies and accessible guidance on the topic. |
| Authority | Author has industry expertise but is not academic. | Strong fit for business/practitioner audiences; relatable authority. |
| Purpose | To inform and persuade - may not be completely objective. | To guide practitioners with actionable, persuasive advice. |
| Context | Illustrates industry framing; good for background reading; weaker for a formal technical report. | Provides accessible, practitioner-focused strategies for a concept brief. |

### Learning points

- The CRAAP test can make it quick and easy to conduct a preliminary
  evaluation.
- Different purposes will shape what makes information useful.

## When you use a source

- Use reliable secondary or published sources to support your claims.
- Cite all sources using the APA 7th edition referencing convention.
- Cross-reference claims to check credibility, especially when using
  AI-generated content.

## Useful library tools

- Library discovery tool (OneSearch) for finding articles and books.
- Google Scholar settings for linking to the library's full-text access.
- Library subject guides for a list of resources for your programme.
