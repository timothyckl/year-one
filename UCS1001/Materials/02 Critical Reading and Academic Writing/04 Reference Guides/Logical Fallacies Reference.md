# Logical Fallacies Reference

A logical fallacy is any flaw or error in reasoning that renders an argument
invalid, unsound, or weak. Fallacies can lead to illogical reasoning and
conclusions, and they can mislead readers. Learn the common ones so you can
avoid them in your own arguments and improve your critical thinking.

| Fallacy | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Ad hominem | "Against the person": remarks against a person's character rather than the person's argument. | During the debate, the candidate's comments attacked his opponent's personal traits rather than the topic being discussed. |
| Ad populum (bandwagon) | "To the people": uses the popularity of a viewpoint to make the reader agree with the majority. | Facebook is used by millions of people to stay connected with various people worldwide. Everyone should get on Facebook. |
| Appeal to authority | Quotes an authority who is not credible for the situation being discussed. | Jane Simmonds has written several story books that appeal to children, so she's an authority on understanding a child's mind. |
| Appeal to ignorance | Believes a claim with no conclusive evidence simply because the writer states the conclusion. | No one has ever proven that UFOs haven't visited Earth yet, so I believe they have. |
| Appeal to pity | Plays on the reader's emotions, winning support by provoking irrelevant feelings of sympathy or pity. | You should not find the defendant guilty of murder, because it will break his poor mother's heart to see him go to jail. |
| Begging the question | Uses circular reasoning by assuming the conclusion in a premise. | This policy is effective because it works well. |
| Equivocation | Shifts a key word between different meanings within the same argument. | A sign says it is "fine" to park here, so parking here must be acceptable. |
| False dichotomy | Presents one or two viewpoints as the only alternatives when many more could be provided. | Some politicians strongly state that universal healthcare is the only option that will allow everyone to have access to healthcare. |
| Hasty generalization | An inaccurate assumption based on an inadequate amount of evidence. | Four out of five college professors prefer to use blue pens to grade. This study is not quite accurate because only five college professors were surveyed. |
| Missing the point | The conclusion does not relate to the main point of the argument. | Drunk driving is an offense that should be given severe punishment. Therefore, there's a need to study the effects of drunk driving. |
| Post hoc (false cause) | Assumes that one cause relates to another cause without sufficient reason. | Walmart retrenched several of its employees. The unemployment rate has increased in the past three months. Walmart caused the unemployment rate to increase. |
| Red herring | Distracts the reader from the main issue being discussed. | A concerned father asks his child about his declining grades and the child quickly speaks of another topic to divert the uncomfortable conversation. |
| Slippery slope | One idea will ultimately lead to many more ideas that result in a final outcome, but not in a logical sequence. | For now, they say they're only interested in banning the carrying of concealed weapons. Before long, though, they'll even try to take away your right to own private property. |
| Straw man | Misrepresents an opponent's argument to persuade the audience to support your point of view. | Guy #1: I think we should give out better study guides to students. Guy #2: I think your idea is bad; we shouldn't give out easy As to everyone. |
| Weak analogy | Compares two ideas or situations that do not relate to one another, weakening the argument. | Guns are like hammers - they both have metal parts and can be used to kill someone. |

## How to avoid fallacies

- Learn the common fallacies so you can spot them in your own and others'
  writing.
- Ask whether the conclusion follows logically from the evidence.
- Check whether emotional appeals, authority, or popularity are being used in
  place of evidence.
- Cross-reference claims before accepting a conclusion.
