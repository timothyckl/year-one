# Week 4: Logical Fallacies

## Overview

A logical fallacy is any flaw or error in reasoning that renders an argument
invalid, unsound, or weak. Recognising common fallacies is a key aspect of
logical thinking.

## What is a logical fallacy?

- Any flaw or error in reasoning that makes an argument invalid, unsound, or
  weak.

### Why do we need to know?

- To avoid fallacies in our own arguments.
- To improve our critical thinking skills.

### How old is the concept?

- The study of fallacies goes back to ancient times - Aristotle identified 13.

### Why avoid fallacies?

- They can lead to illogical reasoning and conclusions.
- They can mislead our readers.

## Common logical fallacies

Each fallacy below is defined with an illustrative example.

### Ad hominem

- Translates as "against the person".
- Refers to the writer making remarks against the person's character rather
  than the person's argument.
- Example: during the debate, the candidate's comments attacked his opponent's
  personal traits rather than the topic being discussed.

### Ad populum (bandwagon)

- Translates as "to the people" or "popular with people"; also known as the
  bandwagon fallacy.
- Refers to a writer using information to get the reader to agree with the
  majority's viewpoint of the argument stated.
- Example: Facebook is used by millions of people to stay connected with
  various people worldwide. Everyone should get on Facebook.

### Appeal to authority

- Used when a writer quotes an authority who is not credible for the situation
  being discussed.
- Example: Jane Simmonds has written several story books that appeal to
  children, so she's an authority on understanding a child's mind.

### Appeal to ignorance

- Leads readers into believing that an argument that has no conclusive evidence
  should be believed simply based on the conclusion stated by the writer.
- Example: no one has ever proven that UFOs haven't visited Earth yet, so I
  believe they have.

### Appeal to pity

- Plays on the reader's emotions; the writer wins the reader's support by
  provoking irrelevant feelings of sympathy or pity.
- Example: you should not find the defendant guilty of murder, because it will
  break his poor mother's heart to see him go to jail.

### Begging the question

- Uses circular reasoning: the argument assumes, in a premise, the conclusion
  that it is supposed to prove.
- Example: this policy is effective because it works well.

### Equivocation

- Uses a key word in two different senses within the same argument, making an
  invalid conclusion appear to follow.
- Example: the sign says it is "fine" to park here; therefore parking here must
  be acceptable. The argument shifts between "fine" as a penalty and "fine" as
  acceptable.

### False dichotomy

- Refers to a writer who presents one or two viewpoints as the only
  alternatives when many more could be provided. This leads the reader into a
  false sense of understanding.
- Example: some politicians strongly state that universal healthcare is the
  only option that will allow everyone to have access to healthcare.

### Hasty generalization

- An inaccurate assumption based on an inadequate amount of evidence.
- Example: four out of five college professors prefer to use blue pens to grade
  rather than red pens. This study is not quite accurate because only five
  college professors were surveyed.

### Missing the point

- Stating an argument in which the conclusion does not relate to the main point
  of the argument.
- Example: drunk driving is an offense that should be given severe punishment.
  Therefore, there's a need to study the effects of drunk driving.

### Post hoc (false cause)

- Means that one cause relates to another cause.
- Example: Walmart retrenched several of its employees. The unemployment rate
  has increased in the past three months. Walmart caused the unemployment rate
  to increase.
- Walmart alone is not the cause of the rise in unemployment; this is a false
  cause. To make the statement stronger, the writer should give more reasons
  that would lead up to this conclusion and include more research to support
  the issue.

### Red herring

- Used to distract the reader from the main issue being discussed.
- Example: a concerned father asks his child about his declining grades in
  school and the child is quick to speak of another topic to divert this
  uncomfortable conversation.

### Slippery slope

- Means that one idea will ultimately lead to many more ideas that result in a
  final outcome, but not in a logical sequence.
- Example: for now, they say they're only interested in banning the carrying of
  concealed weapons. Before long, though, they'll even try to take away your
  right to own private property. It's time to take a stand against these
  communists.

### Straw man

- Used when the writer misrepresents an argument by an opponent to persuade the
  audience to support his point of view.
- Example: Guy #1: I think we should give out better study guides to students.
  Guy #2: I think your idea is bad; we shouldn't give out easy As to everyone.

### Weak analogy

- When someone compares two ideas or two situations that do not relate to one
  another, the argument becomes weak.
- Example: guns are like hammers - they both have metal parts and can be used
  to kill someone.

## How to avoid fallacies

- Learn what the common fallacies are so you can recognise and avoid them.
- Check your own arguments for flawed reasoning before presenting them.
- Cross-reference claims and evaluate the reasoning rather than accepting a
  conclusion at face value.
