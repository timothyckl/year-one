## Reader response (Assignment 2)

- A reader response is an evaluative analysis of a discipline-specific article;
  the task is to critically respond to ONE takeaway from the text.
- Requirements: at least 5 credible secondary sources, cited in APA 7th
  edition.
- Essay structure:
  - Introduction: name the author(s) and article, describe the text, end with
    your thesis/position.
  - Body: explain your analysis; support claims with evidence from the text and
    credible sources; acknowledge counterarguments.
  - Conclusion: restate the thesis, summarise key points, reinforce your
    judgment; no new ideas.
- When critically analysing an idea, research it: what do other writers say?
  What similar and opposing views exist?
- Use the Paul-Elder framework: ten standards of thinking (clarity, accuracy,
  relevance, logic, breadth, precision, significance, completeness, fairness,
  depth) and the elements of thought (purpose, point of view, assumptions,
  implications, data/evidence, concepts, questions).

## Critical reading

- Critical reading is active, deeper engagement with a text: ask questions
  about the content, evaluate claims and evidence, form your own perspective.
- Four stages:
  1. Set a purpose - place the information and check if it is what you need.
  2. Preview - gather basic info (author, title, structure, visuals).
  3. Annotate - thesis, main points, connections, key vocabulary, questions,
     confusing areas.
  4. Evaluate - relevance/validity of claims, evidence and sources,
     assumptions and biases, gaps in reasoning, premature conclusions.

## Synthesis

- Definition: combining information from various sources, coherently and
  logically, to support your point in an essay or report.
- A good synthesis is a dialogue between sources, and between the sources and
  your own ideas.
- Steps: take relevant points, evaluate them, summarise them, plan the order,
  combine them into a paragraph.
- Summary vs synthesis: a summary presents key points from ONE source; a
  synthesis combines main ideas from DIFFERENT sources to provide insight.
- Source integration: in-text citation (idea-focused, author-focused, or
  chronology-focused) plus an end-of-text reference list.

## Logical fallacies

A logical fallacy is a flaw or error in reasoning that renders an argument
invalid, unsound, or weak. Common ones to recognise and avoid:

- Ad hominem (against the person) - attacks character, not argument.
- Ad populum / bandwagon - appeals to the majority.
- Appeal to authority - cites a non-credible authority.
- Appeal to ignorance - believes a claim because it has not been disproven.
- Appeal to pity - plays on emotions instead of evidence.
- Begging the question - assumes the conclusion in a premise, producing
  circular reasoning.
- Equivocation - shifts a key word between different meanings within one
  argument.
- False dichotomy - presents only two options when more exist.
- Hasty generalization - conclusion from inadequate evidence.
- Missing the point - conclusion does not follow the argument's main point.
- Post hoc / false cause - assumes one event caused another.
- Red herring - distracts from the main issue.
- Slippery slope - one step leads to extreme outcomes with no logical chain.
- Straw man - misrepresents an opponent's argument.
- Weak analogy - compares unrelated situations.

## Paraphrasing

- In a paraphrase, you put another writer's ideas into your own words.
- Strategies: highlight main points; use different vocabulary; change sentence
  structure; reorder major ideas where possible; use appropriate synonyms;
  compare versions for sufficient rewording with the same meaning; cite the
  source (APA 7th).
- Never change the meaning (e.g. "the piano with the curved legs" must not
  become "the woman with curved legs").
- Three in-text citation types:
  - Surname + (year) + reporting verb + paraphrase.
  - According to + surname + (year), + paraphrase.
  - Paraphrase (surname, year).

## Flow and cohesion

Three elements of an effective paragraph:

- Unity: one main idea; all sentences support it.
- Coherence: logical order of sentences.
- Cohesion: smooth connection between sentences using cohesive devices.

Strategies to develop continuity:

- Repeat/rephrase key words.
- Place known information before new information.
- Use transitions/logical connectors (for example, in addition, finally).
- Use parallel structure (The Mercury project was designed to ...; The Gemini
  project was intended to ...).
- Use a summary word (this/these + summary noun, e.g. this procedure).

## Summary writing

- A summary is a concise description of the most important information in a
  text: the main idea and its supporting points; it shows your understanding.
- Steps: read closely and repeatedly; annotate; identify main ideas and key
  examples; organise logically; connect with linking words; use your own words.
- Rules: be objective, be concise, paraphrase.
- Reporting verbs (stated, quotes, highlighted, implies, expresses a belief,
  suggests) attribute ideas to the original author.

## Credible sources

- Search strategies: use good synonyms, keep search strings short, be careful
  with truncation, use phrase searching deliberately, and use Booleans
  (AND/OR) correctly. AI tools can help, but human judgement is crucial.
- Match the source to the deliverable: a technical report for academics vs a
  concept brief for business decision-makers.
- Evaluate sources using relevance, authority, purpose, and context (a CRAAP
  test makes this quick for a preliminary evaluation).
