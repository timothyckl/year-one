# Week 2 - Academic Writing

## Overview

This unit develops the building blocks of academic paragraphs and academic
voice. By the end of the lesson you should be able to write an effective topic
sentence and concluding sentence, use relevant supporting details to build a
unified and coherent paragraph, use cohesive devices to connect meanings at
sentence and text level, and edit and proofread for language, tone, and style.

## The Paragraph

A paragraph is made up of three parts, each with a function:

| Part | Function |
|---|---|
| Topic sentence | States what the paragraph is about and the writer's opinion or attitude about it |
| Supporting information | Explains or develops the topic sentence with complete, sufficient, and relevant details |
| Concluding sentence | Restates the main idea and closes the paragraph |

### Topic Sentence

A topic sentence has two parts:

- **Topic**: what the paragraph is about.
- **Controlling idea**: the writer's opinion or attitude about the topic.

A good topic sentence is neither too broad nor too narrow. It should give the
reader a clear sense of what the paragraph will argue or explain.

### Supporting Information

Following the topic sentence, the supporting sentences develop the controlling
idea by giving reasons, causes, and other evidence. A good paragraph is well
developed.

Supporting details include:

- examples
- facts
- other relevant evidence, such as statistics, expert opinion, or reported
  findings

Each supporting sentence should be complete, sufficient, and relevant to the
controlling idea.

### Concluding Sentence

The concluding sentence:

- restates the main idea;
- does not copy the topic sentence;
- includes final thoughts, such as possible implications and suggestions for
  action;
- never introduces a new idea.

## Unity, Coherence, and Cohesion

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Unity | The quality of focus: one idea from start to finish. Every sentence functions to illustrate, clarify, or explain, and thus contributes to the main idea. All information is relevant. |
| Coherence | The logical order of sentences and information, and the connection between the last sentence and the topic sentence. |
| Cohesion | The smooth and logical connection of ideas: the relationship between sentences, achieved through the use of cohesive devices. |

## Editing and Proofreading

- **Editing**: check for correct grammar, spelling, and punctuation; appropriate
  word choices; and a tone and style suited to your audience, purpose, and
  setting.
- **Proofreading**: the final stage of the writing process. Read again to remove
  glaring errors in spelling and punctuation so you present a polished piece of
  writing.

## Tone and Style

### Common Tones

| Tone | Characteristics | Typical uses |
|---|---|---|
| Formal | Professional, serious, objective; sophisticated vocabulary; avoids contractions; follows traditional grammar | Academic papers, business communications, legal documents, official correspondence |
| Informal | Casual, relaxed, conversational; everyday language and contractions | Personal emails, friendly letters, social media |
| Neutral | Objective and unbiased; balanced presentation; avoids strong emotional language or personal opinions | News reporting, scientific writing, technical documentation |
| Persuasive | Influences or convinces the reader; strong language, emotional appeals, rhetorical devices | Advertising, opinion pieces, speeches, persuasive essays |
| Authoritative | Conveys expertise, confidence, credibility; precise language and expert references | Academic writing, expert reports, technical manuals |
| Humorous | Entertains and amuses; wit, irony, wordplay | Satire, comedy writing, some creative writing |

### Common Styles

| Style | Characteristics | Typical uses |
|---|---|---|
| Narrative | Storytelling; descriptive language and vivid details | Novels, short stories, personal narratives |
| Descriptive | Creates a clear, vivid picture using sensory details and imagery | Travel writing, nature descriptions, creative writing |
| Expository | Explains, informs, or describes objectively; facts, evidence, logical organisation | Textbooks, news articles, essays, research papers |
| Persuasive | Convinces the reader to adopt a viewpoint or take action; logical arguments, evidence, emotional appeals | Opinion articles, advertisements, speeches, editorials |
| Argumentative | Presents a clear thesis with evidence; formal and structured; uses reasoning, counterarguments, and refutations | Academic essays and debates |
| Technical | Specialised vocabulary, clarity, and precision | Technical documents, manuals, scientific papers |
| Creative | Artistic expression, literary devices, imaginative storytelling | Poetry, short stories, plays |

Choose tone and style to suit your purpose, audience, and overall message.

## Sentence Structure

A sentence is a string of words that expresses a complete thought. A group of
words that does not express a complete thought is a **fragment**.

| Sentence type | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Simple | One main clause | Machine learning algorithms improve the accuracy of demand forecasting in supply chain management. |
| Compound | Two main clauses joined by a linking conjunction (and, but, or, yet) | Artificial intelligence enhances efficiency in supply chain operations, and it also enables real-time tracking of inventory. |
| Complex | A main clause joined to a subordinate clause (when, because, while) | While integrating blockchain technology into the supply chain can enhance transparency and traceability, it requires careful consideration of security measures. |
| Compound-complex | Two or more independent clauses and at least one dependent clause | The company implemented an advanced inventory management system to reduce stockouts, and they also integrated a real-time tracking system to enhance visibility across the supply chain, which allowed them to address potential bottlenecks proactively. |

## Passive Voice

The passive voice is more common in formal and academic writing than in informal
writing. Unlike the active voice, which focuses on the doer of an action, the
passive voice moves the focus away from the doer.

| Active | Passive |
|---|---|
| The e-commerce platform integrates seamlessly with the inventory management system. | A seamless digital supply chain experience is achieved by the e-commerce platform through seamless integration with the inventory management system. |
| The team revised the delivery schedules. | The delivery schedules were revised and streamlined. |

## Hedges and Boosters

Hedges and boosters are linguistic devices that indicate the degree of certainty
or confidence in presented information. They convey a writer's stance and add
nuance to claims.

### Hedges

Hedges soften a statement and indicate caution or uncertainty. They are used to
acknowledge potential limitations, exceptions, or alternative interpretations.

- Examples: possibly, likely, perhaps, in some cases, it is suggested, it may be
  argued.
- Example sentence: The findings of the study suggest that there may be a
  correlation between these factors.

### Boosters

Boosters strengthen a statement and indicate a high level of certainty or
confidence. They are used to emphasise the importance or significance of a
claim.

- Examples: definitely, certainly, clearly, undoubtedly, it is evident, without
  a doubt.
- Example sentence: The results clearly demonstrate a strong positive
  correlation between these variables.

### Hedging Reporting Verbs

Reporting verbs attribute information to a source and can be modified to
introduce caution or uncertainty.

| Verb | Example |
|---|---|
| Suggest | The study suggests that there may be a correlation between these variables. |
| Indicate | The findings indicate a potential relationship between these factors. |
| Propose | The researcher proposes that this theory could explain the observed phenomenon. |
| Argue | Some scholars argue that this interpretation is plausible but requires further investigation. |
| Claim | The author claims that these results support their hypothesis, although additional research is needed. |

Other hedging verbs: postulate, advise, assume, imply, speculate, tentatively
conclude.

### Booster Verbs

Booster verbs convey assurance and conviction. They should be used in alignment
with the strength of evidence and certainty present in the source.

| Verb | Example |
|---|---|
| Confirm | The study confirms the strong correlation between these variables. |
| Establish | The research establishes a clear link between cause and effect. |
| Demonstrate | The findings demonstrate a significant improvement in the measured outcome. |
| Prove | The experiment proves the validity of the proposed theory. |
| Reveal | The data reveals a striking pattern of behaviour. |
| Underscore | The study underscores the importance of early intervention. |
| Substantiate | The evidence substantiates the claim made by previous researchers. |
| Validate | The study's results validate the effectiveness of the proposed intervention. |

Other booster verbs: corroborate, verify, authenticate, affirm, endorse,
emphasize.

Use hedges and boosters judiciously: they add nuance and accuracy, but they must
reflect the actual level of certainty and evidence in what you report.

## Neutral Reporting Verbs

Neutral reporting verbs present information objectively without conveying a
strong sense of opinion or bias, helping to maintain a balanced and neutral
tone.

- Verbs: state, report, find, note, describe, identify, present, discuss,
  outline, examine, analyse, assess, evaluate, review, investigate, document,
  summarise.

Example sentences:

- The study reports that deep learning algorithms have shown significant
  improvements in image recognition accuracy.
- The analysis describes how natural language processing techniques can enhance
  chatbot interactions and improve customer satisfaction.
- The research paper outlines the potential ethical considerations of using AI
  algorithms in decision-making processes.
- The research paper identifies the challenges of bias and fairness in AI
  algorithms and proposes mitigation strategies.

## Citing Sources As Academic Practice

- Accurately and consistently use in-text and end-of-text citations so readers
  can locate your sources.
- Apply ethical practices for direct quoting, summarising, and paraphrasing, and
  reference accurately to avoid plagiarising.
- List the works you actually used, under a "References" heading, rather than a
  broader "Bibliography".
- The referencing format may differ by discipline or project, so follow the
  format required for the task.

## Editing Checklist

- [ ] Paragraph has a clear topic sentence (topic + controlling idea).
- [ ] Supporting sentences are complete, sufficient, and relevant.
- [ ] Concluding sentence restates the main idea without copying or adding new
      ideas.
- [ ] Paragraph is unified, coherent, and cohesive.
- [ ] Sentences are complete (no fragments) and varied in type.
- [ ] Passive voice is used appropriately for a formal tone.
- [ ] Hedges and boosters match the strength of the evidence.
- [ ] Tone and style suit the audience, purpose, and setting.
- [ ] Grammar, spelling, and punctuation have been checked.
