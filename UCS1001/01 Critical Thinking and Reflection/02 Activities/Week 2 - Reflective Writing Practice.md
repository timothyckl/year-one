# Week 2 - Reflective Writing Practice

## Overview

This activity helps you understand the value of reflection and reflective
writing, write reflectively in response to an experience or your learning, and
see the connection between critical thinking and reflection.

By the end of this activity you should be able to:

- place value in the reflective learning process;
- write reflectively in response to experience and your learning;
- explain the connection between critical thinking and reflection.

## Why Reflection Matters

Reflecting on a learning experience, especially through reflective writing,
plays an important role in university education and beyond. Written reflections
are requested across many disciplines and are also required for workplace
integrated study programmes.

Reflection is linked to significant learning: it fosters a deeper understanding
of a subject and supports professional and personal growth. It moves a person
beyond routine, habitual ways of interpreting the environment toward conscious
consideration of primary experiences.

## Models Of Reflective Thought

### Dewey's Model

Reflective thought is "turning a subject over in the mind and giving it serious
and consecutive consideration." The need for reflection arises when familiar
routines no longer work as a satisfactory way of interacting with the world.
Such a "problem condition" (a disturbance or uncertainty, a habit that does not
work) becomes the springboard for reflective thought and problem-solving. The
result is conscious action and deliberate change rather than a stonewall - the
problem is an opportunity for change and innovation.

### Rodgers' Reflective Cycle

Reflection serves four main purposes:

1. Reflection is a meaning-making process that moves a learner from one
   experience into the next with deeper understanding of its connections to
   other experiences and ideas, making continuity of learning possible.
2. Reflection is a systematic, rigorous, disciplined way of thinking, with
   roots in scientific inquiry.
3. Reflection needs to happen in a community, in interaction with others.
4. Reflection requires attitudes that value the personal and intellectual
   growth of oneself and of others.

### Kolb's Experiential Learning Cycle

Learning is a four-stage cycle:

1. **Concrete experience** - having the experience.
2. **Reflective observation** - reviewing and reflecting on the experience.
3. **Abstract conceptualisation** - drawing conclusions and forming concepts.
4. **Active experimentation** - planning and applying what was learned.

### Critical Reflection

A critical (analytical) reflection goes beyond a purely descriptive reflection.
A writer completes three moves:

- take note of the assumptions that underlie their thoughts and actions;
- evaluate the validity of these assumptions in relation to real-life
  experiences and present contexts;
- act upon what has been learned to more appropriately inform future actions
  and practices.

## Descriptive vs Critical Reflection

| Descriptive reflection | Critical reflection |
|---|---|
| Describes what happened | Describes, then analyses |
| Uses "I" freely | Also examines assumptions, validity, and implications |
| States likes and dislikes | Evaluates the experience against real-life contexts |
| Stops at what was learned | Identifies how learning informs future action |

A descriptive reflection typically covers:

- what I did and why I did it;
- what was good and/or bad about what was done, and why;
- what was easy and/or difficult, and why;
- what I liked or disliked about what I did;
- what other people did and why they did it;
- how what was learned might be used in the future.

A critical reflection adds: what assumptions underlie my thoughts and actions,
how valid are those assumptions given my experiences and context, and what will
I do differently as a result.

## Task 1: Analyse A Problem Scenario

Use the following template to analyse a situation in which a habitual behaviour
no longer seemed effective. Fill in each row.

| Step | Your notes |
|---|---|
| Ideal | The behaviour or outcome you want to achieve |
| Reality (gap) | What actually happens |
| Causes / conditions | The conditions that create the problem |
| Results | The consequences of the current behaviour |
| Goal | What you want instead |
| Possible change | A concrete change you could try |
| Testing the hypothesis | How you would try the change |
| Solution | What happened when you tested it |

Example of a completed scenario:

| Step | Notes |
|---|---|
| Ideal | To stay awake and be proactive during afternoon lectures |
| Reality (gap) | I often feel sleepy during the late afternoon lecture |
| Causes / conditions | The lecture is after lunch; I sleep very late every night; I drink coffee with dinner; I chat late with friends |
| Results | I am a passive student; my understanding of lectures is minimal |
| Goal | To stay awake in class and be more proactive |
| Possible change | Sleep earlier the night before, no coffee with dinner, fewer late-night chats |
| Testing the hypothesis | I decided not to have coffee after dinner |
| Solution | The approach worked; I got to sleep earlier and stayed awake in class for two weeks |

## Task 2: Write A Reflective Response

1. Choose a significant recent experience. Examples: joining a student club,
   travelling alone, learning a new software tool, or trying a new sport.
2. Write the description: what you did and why.
3. Write the interpretation: what was good and/or bad, easy and/or difficult,
   and why; what you liked or disliked; what others did and why.
4. Move to critical reflection: what assumptions underlie your actions, how
   valid are those assumptions, and what would you do differently.
5. State the outcome: what was learned and how it might be used in the future.

## Task 3: Evaluate A Reflection With A Peer

Share your reflection with a partner and evaluate it together. Use the
intellectual standards of the Paul-Elder framework, asking:

- **Clarity**: Is the experience and the thinking behind it clearly explained?
- **Accuracy**: Are the details accurate and specific?
- **Relevance**: Does every part connect to the main experience?
- **Depth**: Does the reflection examine causes, conditions, and complexity?
- **Breadth**: Are other viewpoints (for example, what others did and why)
  considered?
- **Logic**: Do the conclusions follow from the description?
- **Significance**: Is the focus on what matters most about the experience?
- **Fairness**: Are the writer's assumptions examined honestly?

Checklist for peer feedback:

- [ ] The reflection describes the experience and the writer's role in it.
- [ ] It explains why things were good or bad, easy or difficult.
- [ ] It identifies assumptions underlying the writer's thoughts and actions.
- [ ] It evaluates those assumptions against real-life experience.
- [ ] It states what was learned.
- [ ] It identifies a concrete future action or next step.
- [ ] The reflection is written in a clear and coherent structure.

## Self-Reflection

After the activity, answer in your notes:

- Which model (Dewey, Rodgers, Kolb, or critical reflection) best matched the
  way you actually thought about your experience?
- Where was your draft strongest: description, interpretation, or outcome?
- What is the single most important habit you want to keep from the reflective
  writing process?
