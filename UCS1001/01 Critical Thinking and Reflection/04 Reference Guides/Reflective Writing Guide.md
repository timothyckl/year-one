# Reflective Writing Guide

Quick-reference guide for writing reflections that move from description to
critical analysis and future action.

## Why Reflect

- Reflection fosters deeper understanding of a subject and supports personal and
  professional growth.
- Reflective writing is required across many disciplines and in workplace
  integrated learning programmes.
- Reflection moves you beyond routine, habitual interpretation toward conscious
  consideration of your experiences.

## Models Of Reflection

| Model | Key idea | Practical use |
|---|---|---|
| Dewey | A "problem condition" (a habit that does not work) becomes the springboard for reflective thought, leading to conscious action and deliberate change | Use a felt problem as the entry point to analysis |
| Rodgers | Reflection is meaning-making, systematic and disciplined inquiry, social (done with others), and it values personal and intellectual growth | Make reflection rigorous and share it with a community |
| Kolb | Four-stage cycle: concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualisation, active experimentation | Follow all four stages rather than stopping at description |
| Critical reflection | Identify assumptions, evaluate their validity against real-life experience and context, then act to inform future practice | Add the analytical layer to any reflection |

## Descriptive vs Critical Reflection

| Descriptive reflection | Critical reflection |
|---|---|
| Describes what I did and why | Plus: identifies the assumptions behind my thoughts and actions |
| States what was good/bad, easy/difficult, and why | Plus: evaluates whether those assumptions hold in my real-life context |
| States likes and dislikes | Plus: considers implications and alternative viewpoints |
| Notes what others did and why | Plus: draws conclusions and plans action |
| States what was learned | Plus: states what I will do differently in the future |

## Structure: Description - Interpretation - Outcome

### 1. Description (keep it short)

- Past tense for events; present tense for ideas, theories, and models.
- Cover what you did and why, what others did, and the context.

### 2. Interpretation (the most important part)

- Name the significance: "For me, the most significant aspect was..."
- Locate the learning: "My learning arose from...", "happened when...",
  "resulted from..."
- Place it in time: Previously, At first, Initially, Subsequently, Later.
- Name your reaction: "I thought...", "felt...", "noticed...", "questioned...",
  "realised..."
- Explain the cause: "This might be because of...", "due to...", "explained
  by...", "related to..."
- Compare and conclude: "This is similar to...", "This reveals...",
  "demonstrates..."

### 3. Outcome

- Open the change: "Having read/experienced/applied/analysed/learned..."
- State the change: "I now think/feel/realise/know...", "I have learned that..."
- Measure it: "I have significantly/slightly improved my understanding
  of..."
- Acknowledge limits: "However, I have not yet...", "I am not yet confident
  about..."
- State the value: "This knowledge is useful to me as a learner/practitioner
  because..."
- Plan the next step: "This means that...", "As a next step, I need to...",
  "I will now need to..."

## Evaluating A Reflection

Use the intellectual standards of the Paul-Elder framework:

| Standard | Question to ask of the reflection |
|---|---|
| Clarity | Is the experience and the thinking clearly explained? |
| Accuracy | Are the details specific and accurate? |
| Relevance | Does every part connect to the main experience? |
| Depth | Are causes, conditions, and complexity examined? |
| Breadth | Are other viewpoints (what others did and why) considered? |
| Logic | Do the conclusions follow from the description? |
| Significance | Does the reflection focus on what matters most? |
| Fairness | Are the writer's assumptions examined honestly? |

## Checklist For A Complete Reflection

- [ ] Describes the experience and the writer's role in it.
- [ ] Explains why things were good or bad, easy or difficult.
- [ ] Names what the writer liked or disliked and why.
- [ ] Notes what other people did and why.
- [ ] Identifies the assumptions underlying the writer's thoughts and actions.
- [ ] Evaluates those assumptions against real-life experience and context.
- [ ] States what was learned.
- [ ] Identifies a concrete future action or next step.
- [ ] Uses appropriate tense (past for events, present for ideas).
- [ ] Applies the intellectual standards to check quality.
